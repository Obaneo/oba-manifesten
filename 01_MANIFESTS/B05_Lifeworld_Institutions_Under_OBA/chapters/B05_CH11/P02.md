# B05.CH11.P02 — Diagnostics: where food becomes L (and where E grows)

This paragraph specifies the diagnostic model for food and basic essentials under OBA: where food becomes a dominant lifeworld burden
(L) and where extraction (E) tends to grow. Food becomes L when essentials-basket prices rise faster than incomes, when access gaps
form (regional deserts, transport barriers), and when shocks propagate through concentrated chains and input dependencies. E grows when
market power concentrates in processing, retail, and input suppliers; when intermediaries build fee-stacks through trading and
“services”; and when contract structures impose clamp on producers and workers through exclusivity, late payments, coercive terms, and
slotting fees. The diagnostic purpose is operational: it defines mechanism families that later paragraphs must neutralise via chain
decomposition, price rails, commons logistics/reserves, contract standards, and fail-closed escalation under shocks.

---

<a id="b05ch11p02s01"></a>
## B05.CH11.P02.S01 — Concentration: oligopoly in processing, retail, input suppliers → market power and extreme margins

Concentration converts food chains into toll ecosystems: essential demand meets limited contestability.

### Concentration surfaces (binding)
- **Processing oligopoly**: limited number of processors controlling bottleneck stages (slaughter, milling, dairy, packaging), enabling
  margin expansion and supplier coercion.
- **Retail concentration**: dominant retail chains control shelf access, pricing, and promotions; can impose slotting fees and late
  payments.
- **Input supplier concentration**: fertiliser, seeds/IP, agrochemicals, and machinery providers can impose licensing tolls and bundling.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Rising mark-ups and margin dispersion not explained by input costs; persistent price-to-cost divergence in staple categories.
- Supplier dependence (single-buyer regions), reduced farmgate share, and increased retailer/processor profitability during price spikes.
- High HHI/concentration metrics and rising barriers to entry (exclusive contracts, capacity constraints).

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Where concentration correlates with extreme margins or coercive terms, structural remedies (unbundling, public options, procurement
  shifts) become mandatory candidates.

<a id="b05ch11p02s02"></a>
## B05.CH11.P02.S02 — Intermediary layers: brokers, trading desks, “services” that build fee-stacks

Intermediary ecosystems expand when chains are opaque and volatile; “risk management” can become rent.

### Fee-stack mechanisms (binding)
- **Broker layers**: mandatory brokers between producers and processors/retailers that capture spreads without improving outcomes.
- **Trading desks**: speculative or quasi-speculative intermediation that increases price volatility or captures spreads through opacity.
- **Service layers**: compliance consulting, certification brokerage, logistics “platform” tolls, and data services sold back to chain
  participants.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Rising spread between farmgate and consumer prices without corresponding logistics/processing cost increases.
- Proliferation of fees (brokerage, certification, platform access, “market data”) and increased contract complexity.
- High share of chain value captured by entities not directly adding productive capacity (P).

### Requalification trigger (binding)
- Where intermediary revenues grow without measurable improvements in reliability, waste reduction, or quality, the layer is treated as
  E and triggers decomposition and removal/optionalisation.

<a id="b05ch11p02s03"></a>
## B05.CH11.P02.S03 — Contract clamp: exclusive offtake, late payments, supply-chain coercion, slotting fees

Contract terms can impose clamp even when headline prices seem stable. OBA treats coercive contracting as a core E mechanism.

### Clamp mechanisms (binding)
- **Exclusive offtake**: forcing producers into single-buyer dependence without fair price rails or exit options.
- **Late payments**: shifting working capital burden onto suppliers; payment delays function as hidden financing extraction.
- **Supply-chain coercion**: unilateral contract changes, retroactive penalties, quality disputes used opportunistically, forced rebates.
- **Slotting fees and shelf access tolls**: payments required for placement, promotions, or retention; used to extract from suppliers and
  exclude entrants.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Increasing late-payment days, rising dispute/chargeback incidence, contract churn with unilateral amendments, and growth of slotting
  and “marketing contribution” fees.
- Supplier exit, reduced diversity of suppliers, and increasing dependence on intermediaries to secure shelf access.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Detected clamp patterns trigger contract standards, payment-time norms, and audit escalation; repeat offenders face licence/procurement
  consequences.

<a id="b05ch11p02s04"></a>
## B05.CH11.P02.S04 — Shock channels: energy prices, climate, geopolitics, transport, disease outbreaks

Food systems are shock-coupled: non-food shocks translate into price spikes and shortages.

### Shock classes (binding)
- **Energy prices**: fuel and electricity costs affect fertiliser, processing, cold chain, and transport; amplify price volatility.
- **Climate variability**: droughts, floods, heat waves, and crop failures; seasonal shifts and yield instability.
- **Geopolitics**: export restrictions, sanctions, shipping disruptions, conflict affecting key producers.
- **Transport and logistics**: port congestion, container shortages, labour strikes, cold-chain failures.
- **Disease outbreaks**: livestock disease, crop pests, and food safety crises disrupting supply.

### Translation to L and R/B tails (binding)
- Shocks become lifeworld harm when reserves are thin, logistics capacity lacks redundancy, and pricing lacks transparency; volatility
  passes through to households without baseline protections.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Price spikes correlated with shortages and rationing; increasing lead times; rising stockout incidents; and increased waste due to
  logistics fragility.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Repeated shocks without resilience improvement indicate governance failure; triggers include reserve build-up, commons logistics
  investment, and emergency regimes with strict sunset and post-mortem audits (fail-closed against chronic tail exposure).
