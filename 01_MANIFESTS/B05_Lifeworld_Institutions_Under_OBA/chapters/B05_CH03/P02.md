# B05.CH03.P02 — Canonical framing: care as infrastructure, time as scarce allocation

This paragraph establishes the canonical framing for CH03: **care is infrastructure** and **time is a scarce allocation variable**.
In the lifeworld layer, time is often the binding constraint that converts nominal rights and nominal income into non-usable access.
If time sovereignty is low, institutions can “improve” outcomes on paper while increasing hidden burdens (L): travel time, waiting
time, scheduling chaos, administrative proof burdens, and crisis-driven coordination.

Care is therefore treated as **commons capacity**: a set of services, roles, and local infrastructures whose outputs are testable as
access, quality, continuity, and contestability—under privacy minimalism. Social reproduction is not sentiment; it is a stabiliser of
R/B tails. When care collapses, tails worsen: dropouts, burnout, household debt spirals, and long-run capacity loss.

The framing is explicitly non-moralising: OBA does not prescribe who “should” provide care. It sets an institutional boundary: the
order must reduce frictions and expand choice space so that care can be provided (formally or informally) without coercion and without
clamp dynamics.

---

## B05.CH03.P02.S01 — Time as the primary constraint: income growth without time sovereignty increases hidden L

Time is treated as the primary constraint because it mediates every other resource. Income gains that arrive with schedule capture,
waiting queues, and administrative burden can *reduce* effective autonomy even while N rises.

### Time constraint mechanism
- **Schedule capture**: unpredictable shifts, on-call domination, and short-notice changes destroy planning capacity and increase
  childcare/transport costs (hidden L).
- **Queue and travel time**: long waits for childcare/care/health services function as rationing; the “price” is time.
- **Administrative time burden**: proof requirements, re-assessments, and fragmented interfaces externalise workload to households.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any claim of improvement in welfare or autonomy MUST include time-friction signals (predictability, travel time, waiting time,
  admin burden) as part of the lifeworld test; cash-only measures are insufficient.
- Policies that raise time burdens while increasing nominal income MUST be treated as suspect: they often shift L from money to time.

## B05.CH03.P02.S02 — Care as commons capacity: access, quality, and continuity as testable outputs

Care is framed as infrastructure: it must be provisioned, maintained, staffed, and governed with the same seriousness as physical
systems—because failure produces immediate lifeworld harm.

### Commons-capacity outputs (testable)
- **Access**: coverage, eligibility clarity, affordability (including time affordability), non-discrimination.
- **Quality**: safety, effectiveness, continuity of carers/teachers, dignity, incident discipline.
- **Continuity**: service reliability over time; staffing adequacy; backlog control; crisis resilience.
- **Contestability**: complaint and appeal rails; correction procedures; publication of aggregated performance.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any care system claiming commons status MUST publish service minima, backlog indicators, staffing ratios, and incident handling,
  within privacy rails.
- Any private fee-stack that gates care access (paid priority, brokerage, opaque insurance conditions) MUST be treated as extraction
  and is non-conforming unless neutralised or requalified under explicit rails.

## B05.CH03.P02.S03 — Social reproduction as an R/B stabiliser: less crisis-driven dropout and tail-risk

Social reproduction stabilises tails by preventing small shocks from becoming life-course collapses. Care capacity is therefore a
first-order R/B stabiliser, not a “nice-to-have.”

### Stabiliser mechanism
- **Reduced crisis dropout**: childcare continuity prevents job loss; care support prevents caregiver burnout and forced exit from work.
- **Lower tail-risk**: fewer arrears cascades and emergency borrowing caused by sudden care gaps.
- **Capability preservation**: sustained learning and health capacity reduces long-term productivity loss and institutional strain.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Care/time policies MUST be evaluated on tail outcomes (time poverty, dropout rates, crisis incidence), not only on average service
  usage or median satisfaction.
- If reforms increase crisis-driven reliance on households (more unpaid coordination, more emergency gaps), they worsen tails and are
  non-conforming unless corrected with buffers and measurable improvements.

---

## B05.CH03.P02.S04 — Boundary: no moral obligation to provide care; institutions must reduce frictions and expand choice space

OBA’s framing is not a moral doctrine about family roles. The boundary is institutional: the system must not rely on coerced unpaid
care as an invisible subsidy.

### Boundary principles
- **No role prescription**: do not encode gendered or cultural assumptions about who should provide care.
- **Choice space expansion**: enable multiple care arrangements by reducing time and administrative frictions and by providing reliable
  baseline services.
- **Non-coercion**: do not use benefit regimes, surveillance, or stigma to force caregiving decisions.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Care rights and support must be accessible with minimal proof burden and must not impose humiliating or behaviour-steering controls.
- If policy design results in systematic care coercion (households must provide care because no alternative exists), the order is
  externalising L and must be treated as drifting/incomplete.
