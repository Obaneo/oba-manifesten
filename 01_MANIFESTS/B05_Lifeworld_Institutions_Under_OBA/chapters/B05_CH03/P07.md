# B05.CH03.P07 — Security around care: rights, funding, and administrative frictions

This paragraph specifies the security architecture around care: **care rights**, **funding rails**, and the elimination of
administrative friction as hidden L. In OBA terms, rights that exist only on paper—because access requires time, shame, repetitive
proof, or opaque discretion—are not rights. They are clamp mechanisms that externalise burdens to households and create a bureaucratic
toll ecosystem (paperwork-as-E).

The objective is to make care security **portable, low-friction, and non-stigmatising**. Rights must be parameterisable (countries
choose package breadth), but the rails are invariant: minimal proof burden, standardised procedures, contestability, and no reliance
on private insurance clamp or fee-stacks for baseline access.

---

## B05.CH03.P07.S01 — Care rights: who is entitled to what, without stigma (parameterisable)

Care rights define baseline entitlements: access to childcare, respite care, long-term care support, and related services. OBA allows
parameter choices (universal vs targeted), but requires that entitlement logic is explicit and non-stigmatising.

### Entitlement design (parameter space)
- **Universal baseline**: broad entitlement with scarcity rules and prioritisation under capacity constraints.
- **Targeted baseline**: eligibility based on income, disability, caregiver burden, or household structure, with strict anti-stigma rails.

### Anti-stigma and dignity rails (binding)
- Rights must not be framed or administered as moral judgment.
- Eligibility criteria must be transparent and appealable; discretionary “worthiness” assessments are non-conforming.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Entitlements MUST be published as rule sets (machine-readable where feasible), including what is covered, wait-time norms, and
  escalation triggers for capacity breaches.
- Any regime where users avoid access due to humiliation, fear, or administrative hostility is failing the lifeworld test and must be
  corrected.

---

## B05.CH03.P07.S02 — Friction reduction: one-stop interface, standard procedures, minimal proof burden

Administrative friction is a direct time burden and thus a hidden L component. The care system must therefore be designed around
low-friction execution.

### Friction reduction mechanisms (binding)
- **One-stop interface**: a single access point for care rights where possible, with clear case status visibility.
- **Standard procedures**: uniform forms and criteria; avoid local improvisation that produces postcode lotteries.
- **Minimal proof burden**: use pre-fill and trusted data sources where legitimate; do not require repeated re-proving of stable facts.
- **Time-bounded decisions**: maximum lead times for decisions and appeals; breach triggers provisional access and escalation.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- If uptake drop-off or appeal backlogs rise due to administrative burden, the regime must be treated as non-functional and must be
  redesigned (process simplification is not optional).
- Care access must be feasible for low-capacity households (language barriers, disability, limited time), not only for “good form fillers.”

## B05.CH03.P07.S03 — Prohibited bureaucratic toll: paperwork-as-E (requalification)

Bureaucratic tolling occurs when paperwork, verification loops, and intermediaries become an industry that extracts time and money
while claiming to “ensure compliance.” Under OBA, this is treated as E when it is primarily a gatekeeping ecosystem rather than a
necessary integrity function.

### Paperwork-as-E indicators (non-exhaustive)
- Repeated re-assessments without new information; redundant documentation across agencies.
- Private intermediaries paid to navigate public rights (“access brokers”).
- Complex eligibility designs that require professional assistance to claim basic entitlements.
- Long decision cycles used as rationing rather than explicit scarcity rules.

### Requalification rule (binding)
- Processes that generate structural friction without proportional integrity benefit must be redesigned or requalified as E and
  neutralised (simplification, automation under audit, or elimination).
- “Complexity as a feature” (to deter uptake) is explicitly non-conforming.

## B05.CH03.P07.S04 — Transition: from private insurance clamp to public/commons rails (link to CH09)

Where care security is mediated by private insurance or quasi-public fee-stack ecosystems, the system often produces clamp:
network restrictions, denial incentives, administrative games, and paid priority access. OBA requires a transition toward public/commons
rails for baseline care access, with private layers (if any) constrained to non-clamp add-ons.

### Transition logic (binding)
- **Baseline access** must be secured through public/commons mechanisms: transparent coverage, standard tariffs, and appealable decisions.
- **Private add-ons** (if allowed) must not create lock-in, must not gate baseline access, and must not rely on opaque exclusion.
- **Auditability**: denial patterns, wait-time outcomes, and fee-stack shares must be measurable and contestable (link CH09 anti-toll rails).

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- If private insurance clamp is required to access basic care, the system is non-conforming unless a transition plan exists with
  measurable milestones, sunsets for clamp clauses, and a clear public option.
- Any transition must be fail-closed against vendor capture: procurement and system tooling must remain exitable and auditable.
