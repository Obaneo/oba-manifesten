# B05.CH03.P05 — Informal caregiving and long-term care as lifeworld essentials

This paragraph specifies informal caregiving and long-term care as **lifeworld essentials**: domains where failure produces immediate
lock-in, hidden burdens (L), and R/B tail blowups. OBA treats caregiving not as a private moral obligation but as a structural
interface: households will provide informal care in many cases, but the order must not rely on unbounded unpaid care as an invisible
subsidy. Where care needs exceed household capacity, institutions must provide commons capacity and buffers to prevent collapse.

The design goals are: (1) formally recognise informal care as hidden L without monetising it into a reductionist KPI, (2) build care
commons that provide respite and continuity without clamp dynamics, (3) enforce contestability and non-discrimination, and (4) treat
sudden care demand as a shock class with fallback commons (CH04).

---

## B05.CH03.P05.S01 — Informal care as hidden L: time costs and mental load (do not monetise, but formally recognise)

Informal caregiving imposes time costs, emotional labour, coordination load, and opportunity loss. These burdens are often invisible
in economic indicators and therefore become an easy dumping ground for institutional “efficiency.”

### Recognition logic
- **Hidden L**: caregiving burdens are treated as lifeworld burdens that reduce residual space (R) and increase tail-risk.
- **Non-monetisation**: OBA does not require converting caregiving into money equivalents; monetisation often invites coercive
  optimisation and surveillance.
- **Formal recognition**: systems must acknowledge caregiving as a legitimate constraint in eligibility, scheduling, and support
  design, rather than treating it as personal failure.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Policies affecting work and social rights MUST treat caregiving constraints as valid and must not penalise caregivers via
  impossible requirements (time burdens, proof burdens, schedule capture).
- Measurement of caregiving burdens MUST use bounded signals (time-friction proxies, access gaps, waiting time) under privacy rails,
  not continuous monitoring.

---

## B05.CH03.P05.S02 — Care commons: respite care, neighbourhood teams, home-care rails, access without clamp dynamics

Care commons must provide real capacity so informal care does not become coercive necessity. The focus is on **respite**,
**continuity**, and **access without clamp**.

### Commons components (non-exhaustive)
- **Respite care**: temporary relief services that prevent burnout and crisis escalation.
- **Neighbourhood care teams**: locally accessible support units that reduce travel time and administrative fragmentation.
- **Home-care rails**: standardised pathways for in-home support, assistive devices, and adaptations, with maintenance discipline.
- **Continuity rails**: stable caregiver assignment where possible; handover protocols; incident discipline.

### Anti-clamp requirements (binding)
- Access must not depend on employer permission, private insurance gatekeeping, or broker intermediaries.
- No fee-stacks: prohibit paid priority, opaque surcharges, or “management layers” that turn care into a toll market.
- Portability: care access and entitlements must follow the person across region and provider changes within explicit boundary rules.

---

## B05.CH03.P05.S03 — Contestability: complaint procedures, waiting times, non-discrimination audits

Care is high-stakes. Contestability is therefore constitutive: users must be able to challenge decisions, and systems must be able to
detect structural exclusion and quality decay.

### Contestability infrastructure (binding)
- **Complaint and appeal procedures**: time-bounded, low-friction pathways with clear escalation ladders.
- **Waiting time rails**: maximum lead times by care pathway; breaches trigger automatic escalation and capacity intervention.
- **Non-discrimination audits**: detect exclusion by region, income, disability status, language, or administrative capacity.
- **Publication**: aggregated tables on coverage, wait times, incident rates, staffing ratios, and appeal outcomes (join-safe).

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- If waiting times are “normalised” without escalation, care becomes effectively unavailable and hidden L rises (non-conforming).
- If appeals are slow, opaque, or retaliatory, contestability fails and the system must be treated as clamp-producing.

---

## B05.CH03.P05.S04 — Crisis buffer: sudden care demand as a shock → fallback commons (link to CH04)

Care shocks are predictable tail events: sudden illness, disability, accident, or family breakdown can rapidly destroy household
capacity. OBA treats this as a shock class requiring buffers and fallback commons, not moral judgment.

### Shock buffer logic (binding)
- **Trigger recognition**: sudden care demand events activate fast-track pathways (no prolonged proof cycles).
- **Fallback commons**: emergency care continuity, temporary respite, rapid equipment/adaptation access, and legal/administrative
  assistance as needed.
- **Financial/time buffers**: temporary leave rights, income stabilisers, and scheduling protections to prevent cascades into job loss
  and debt.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- If care shock response relies on household improvisation and private debt, the order is externalising tails and is non-conforming.
- Shock regimes must be time-bounded and auditable (CH04): triggers, instruments used, outcomes, and restatements where failures occur.
