# B05.CH03.P12 — Failure modes and hard stops

This paragraph specifies the primary failure modes of the time/care/social reproduction layer and defines hard-stop rules. CH03 is
highly vulnerable to drift because it sits at the intersection of intimate life, public services, labour markets, and data systems.
Failure modes here are systemic: measurement appetite expands, capacity decays under budget pressure, and capture incentives arise
through privatisation, vendor lock-in, and political control of access.

Hard stops are lifeworld-based: if care access and quality deteriorate structurally, if administrative tolling grows, or if the system
requires surveillance to function, the implementation is non-conforming and must be redesigned or rolled back. “Good intentions” do
not override these rails.

---

## B05.CH03.P12.S01 — Over-measurement: care as a data factory → surveillance and burnout (non-conform)

Over-measurement occurs when care governance drifts into the belief that “more data equals better care.” This expands into surveillance
rent and execution overload: staff time is consumed by reporting, households are forced into proof cycles, and vendors gain leverage
through tooling.

### Failure mechanism
- **Data maximalism**: increasing variables, deeper joins, more frequent reporting, expanded identifiers.
- **Surveillance drift**: monitoring becomes a condition for access or “quality assurance,” creating behavioural coercion.
- **Burnout and capacity loss**: professionals spend time on documentation rather than care; administrative L rises.

### Conformance rule (binding)
- Care measurement MUST be privacy-minimal and bounded (CH03.P09/P11). If a design requires pervasive tracking or exhaustive
  documentation, it is **non-conforming**.
- If reporting load reduces service capacity or increases waiting times, measurement scope MUST be reduced and redesigned (fail-closed
  against data-factory drift).

---

## B05.CH03.P12.S02 — Maintenance collapse: staffing shortage/backlogs → capacity loss and legitimacy breach

Care systems fail primarily through maintenance collapse: understaffing, training bottlenecks, backlog accumulation, and incident
normalisation. This produces a legitimacy breach: “rights” exist but are unreachable.

### Failure mechanism
- **Structural understaffing**: ratios below minima; high churn; vacancy durations grow.
- **Backlog growth**: waiting times breach maxima; unmet needs rise; crisis cases crowd out baseline provision.
- **Incident normalisation**: safety and quality failures are hidden, underreported, or treated as “the new normal.”

### Hard stop (binding)
- Persistent breach of staffing minima or waiting time rails without a published remediation plan triggers capacity-breach status and
  escalation.
- If backlogs grow structurally (ΔG analogue negative) and are not reversed, the system must be treated as failing the lifeworld test
  and cannot claim compatibility.

---

## B05.CH03.P12.S03 — Capture: private fee-stacks or political hijacking of care commons → hard stop

Care commons are prime capture targets. Capture reintroduces extraction or turns access into a political instrument—both are
incompatible with OBA.

### Capture forms
- **Private fee-stacks**: paid priority, brokerage layers, opaque insurance gating, “management” intermediaries, vendor-controlled
  scheduling and access.
- **Commercial data capture**: profiling, targeting, or monetisation based on care data.
- **Political hijacking**: selective access, ideological gatekeeping, patronage allocation, suppression of incident reporting.

### Hard stop (binding)
- The emergence of fee-stack gating around baseline care access is a hard stop: it must trigger requalification/remedy (contract
  rewrite, unbundling, public option expansion) or suspension of the programme’s legitimacy claims.
- Proven political control of access or suppression of audits/incidents triggers governance reset protocols (“audit of the auditor”),
  and compatibility claims must be suspended pending remediation.

---

## B05.CH03.P12.S04 — Fail-closed: if access/quality structurally worsens or administrative toll grows, redesign is mandatory

The lifeworld layer is defined by executable tests. When those tests show deterioration, the system must not rationalise harm as
transition cost or “implementation learning” without controlled rollback.

### Fail-closed conditions (binding)
Redesign (or rollback/suspension) is mandatory if any of the following occur without an external shock explanation:
- **Access worsens**: lower coverage, longer waits, higher drop-off, more exclusions.
- **Quality worsens**: rising incidents, declining continuity, increased complaint volumes with slow resolution.
- **Administrative toll grows**: more proof steps, longer decision cycles, higher appeal backlog, increased time burden for households.
- **Selective execution**: cohort/region divergences that are hidden or normalised rather than corrected.

### Required response (binding)
- Publish restatements and corrective action plans with timelines and measurable targets.
- Reduce scope to what can be executed under privacy and capacity constraints.
- If observability fails (no reliable measurement without surveillance), the system must fail-closed: no compatibility claim and no
  scaling until redesigned.
