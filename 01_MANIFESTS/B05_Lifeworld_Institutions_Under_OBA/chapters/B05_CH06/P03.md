# B05.CH06.P03 — Credit as a lifeworld interface: where the clamp forms

This paragraph specifies how credit operates as a **lifeworld interface** and where the **contract clamp** typically forms. Credit is
not neutral plumbing: it is a coupling layer between (i) essential access systems, (ii) household cashflow timing, and (iii) creditor
control and enforcement capacity. Clamp emerges when the borrower’s exit options are structurally weak (essentials dependence,
scarcity, time pressure) and contract design converts that weakness into durable lock-in and extraction.

OBA therefore treats certain credit domains as high-risk by default (essentials credit), defines canonical clamp clauses and pathways
(break fees, refinancing friction, variable-rate risk without hedge, cross-default), requalifies the collections ecosystem as a
fee-stack surface (E), and treats digital credit rails as a combined extraction+manipulation channel (dark patterns + data toll).

---

## B05.CH06.P03.S01 — Essentials credit: housing/energy/health as L domains with high clamp risk

Essentials credit is structurally prone to clamp because the underlying service is non-optional and often scarce. Households will
accept worse terms to avoid losing shelter, heat, or care continuity.

### High-risk domains (binding presumption)
- **Housing**: mortgages, rent deposits, arrears lending, “renovation loans” tied to housing scarcity.
- **Energy**: arrears financing, pay-as-you-go penalties, disconnection-linked advance payments.
- **Health**: out-of-pocket financing, insurance-related credit, payment plans where care access is leveraged.

### Clamp mechanism
- Scarcity and dependency reduce exit power; creditors can price in vulnerability and impose punitive enforcement.
- The credit channel becomes an indirect allocator of essentials access, which is incompatible unless governed by explicit rails.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Essentials credit must meet stricter admissibility: all-in pricing, fee caps, clause prohibitions, fast dispute flow, and protection
  against disconnection/eviction cascades.
- Where essentials dependence cannot be protected via contract rails alone, a commons/public option must provide exit paths (link CH04/CH07/CH08/CH09).

## B05.CH06.P03.S02 — Contract clamp: break fees, refinancing friction, variable rates without hedge, cross-default

Clamp is often produced by a small set of recurring contract devices that convert “choice” into dependency.

### Canonical clamp devices (non-exhaustive)
- **Break/exit fees**: punitive early repayment charges, termination penalties, “administration fees” as disguised break fees.
- **Refinancing friction**: procedural barriers, reset windows, hidden repricing, portability failure, forced bundling of new products.
- **Variable-rate exposure without hedge**: rate resets that shift macro risk onto households without a usable hedge or fair conversion
  option.
- **Cross-default and cross-collateralisation**: one missed payment triggers defaults across products; collateral grabs become
  disproportionate.
- **Bundled dependency**: tying credit to insurance, service contracts, or platform lock-ins that amplify L.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Standard contracts must cap or prohibit clamp clauses; where clauses remain, they must be explicit, bounded, and contestable.
- Borrowers must have real exit: portability, refinancing access, and a dispute mechanism that can pause enforcement pending review.

## B05.CH06.P03.S03 — Collections ecosystem: fees, penalties, bailiff costs as a fee-stack (E)

Collections commonly turns small arrears into long-run clamp by stacking costs and accelerating enforcement.

### Fee-stack pattern
- **Penalty cascades**: late fees + penalty interest + “reminder fees” + collection agency fees + court/bailiff costs.
- **Volume incentives**: third-party collectors profit from escalation, not resolution.
- **Coercive leverage**: threats and procedural complexity extract payment under distress rather than restoring solvency.

### Requalification (binding)
- Collections add-ons that are not functional cost recovery are classified as **E** and must be capped, banned, or converted into a
  public/commons enforcement rail with bounded costs and strict conduct rules.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Enforced cost stacks must be all-in disclosed and bounded; debtors must have a fast dispute/objection path with enforceable pauses.
- If collections economics rely on vulnerability exploitation, the regime is non-conforming and must be redesigned toward dignified
  reset and restructuring (link P10/P11).

## B05.CH06.P03.S04 — Digital credit rails: BNPL/fintech dark patterns, data extraction as a toll (E)

Digital credit products often fuse two extraction mechanisms: (i) behavioural manipulation (dark patterns) and (ii) data extraction
as rent.

### Clamp and extraction mechanisms
- **Dark patterns**: frictionless sign-up, hidden repayment schedules, penalty-by-default design, gamified spending, misleading “free”
  framing.
- **Data toll**: profiling for pricing and steering; secondary use of transaction data; paid “privacy” as a product tier.
- **Embedded lock-in**: credit tied to platform ecosystems, app stores, identity lockouts, or payment rail gating.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- BNPL and fintech credit must meet strict disclosure (all-in APR equivalent, fees, penalties) and must prohibit manipulative UI that
  shifts consent into default.
- Data use must be minimal and purpose-limited; profiling for price discrimination or behavioural steering is treated as E and is
  non-conforming.
- If a digital rail cannot be made auditable and non-manipulative, it must be restricted or banned; public/commons alternatives must
  provide exit.
