# B05.CH06.P02 — Canonical distinctions: productive debt versus parasitic debt

This paragraph defines the canonical distinction between **productive debt** and **parasitic debt** under OBA. The distinction is
functional and lifeworld-bound: debt is productive when it finances **real capacity** (P or commons/ΔG) under bounded and transparent
terms that are serviceable from expected cashflows without clamp. Debt is parasitic when its economics rely on rollovers, penalties,
fee-stacks, collateral/asset inflation, and lock-in—i.e., when repayment capacity is replaced by leverage over the debtor’s future
options.

The classification is not moral; it is operational. It determines admissibility, pricing rails, enforcement constraints, and when
restructuring or prohibition is mandatory. Where cases are mixed (notably mortgages and education), classification depends on price
discipline, baseline access, and whether contracts create clamp. Evidence discipline is central: the party benefiting from asymmetry
carries the burden of proof.

---

## B05.CH06.P02.S01 — Productive: finances P or commons capacity (ΔG) with bounded returns and clear cashflow coverage

Productive debt is credit that funds capacity and can be serviced without coercive dependence.

### Functional criteria (productive; binding)
- **Use-of-funds**: finances P (capex, working capital, maintenance) or commons capacity (ΔG-like infrastructure and service delivery).
- **Cashflow coverage**: repayment is plausibly covered by cashflows generated by the financed activity (or by stable, non-clamping
  income), without dependence on refinancing as a business model.
- **Bounded returns**: interest and fees correspond to functional costs (funding + risk + operations), with caps on spreads and junk
  fees.
- **Transparent terms**: all-in APR disclosure, standard contract formats, clear triggers, and portability where relevant.
- **No clamp clauses**: no punitive exit barriers, cross-default traps, coercive collateral grabs beyond proportional remedies.

### Conformance requirement (binding)
- A product classified as productive must ship with an audit pack: use-of-funds logic, pricing decomposition, default pathways, and
  evidence that expected repayment does not depend on penalties or asset inflation.

---

## B05.CH06.P02.S02 — Parasitic debt: rolls over, stacks fees/interest, leans on collateral/asset inflation, increases L and lock-in

Parasitic debt is debt whose profitability depends on debtor vulnerability, opacity, and lock-in rather than productive outcomes.

### Functional criteria (parasitic; binding)
- **Rollover dependence**: the model expects repeated refinancing/extension; principal is not realistically amortised.
- **Fee/penalty stacking**: revenue relies on junk fees, late fees, penalty interest, collections add-ons, and cascading charges.
- **Collateral/asset inflation dependence**: repayment or profitability relies on rising asset prices (or forced collateral liquidation),
  rather than on cashflow from productive use.
- **Lock-in and coercion**: contract terms or enforcement pathways reduce exit power (break fees, blacklisting, aggressive garnishment,
  cross-default triggers).
- **L amplification**: increases lifeworld burdens through volatility, arrears cascades, and administrative friction.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Parasitic debt must be neutralised: requalification as E components triggers redesign (fee caps, clause prohibitions), restructuring,
  or product bans. If the structure cannot be made auditable and bounded, it is non-conforming by default.

## B05.CH06.P02.S03 — Mixed cases: mortgage/education — classification depends on price discipline, access, and contract clamp

Some debt categories can be productive in principle but parasitic in practice due to institutional design (scarcity, pricing power,
and policy choices).

### Mortgage (mixed)
- Productive only if credit operates under **price discipline** (no demand-stimulus asset support), bounded LTV, transparent fees, and
  portability/refinancing without punitive friction.
- Becomes parasitic when cheap credit + scarcity inflates prices, raising L and making households dependent on asset inflation and
  refinancing cycles.

### Education/student finance (mixed)
- Productive only if education functions as a commons-like capacity builder: baseline access limits debt reliance; repayment is bounded,
  income-contingent, and does not clamp career choices.
- Becomes parasitic when debt functions as discipline (choice coercion), compounds over life, or relies on penalties and administrative
  gatekeeping.

### Conformance rule (binding)
- Mixed cases are classified by outcome and clamp: if the product increases essentials-L, creates lock-in, or depends on asset/claim
  support channels, it must be treated as parasitic and redesigned.

## B05.CH06.P02.S04 — Evidence rule: whoever profits from information or power asymmetry carries the burden of proof (audit pack)

Credit markets are asymmetry machines. OBA therefore assigns proof burden to the side best able to control and exploit asymmetry.

### Evidence rule (binding)
- The lender/intermediary (or the party extracting fees) must provide the audit pack proving:
  - full cost disclosure (all-in APR; fee decomposition),
  - non-clamping contract structure,
  - serviceability without rollovers/penalties as the core revenue model,
  - and lawful, contestable enforcement pathways.

### Fail-closed application
- Missing disclosure, opaque securitisation, or unverifiable pricing/eligibility logic triggers conservative presumptions: classify fee
  components as E, restrict the product, require redesign, or ban.
- Borrower inability to prove harm is not a defence for opacity; lack of evidence due to asymmetry counts against the extractor.
