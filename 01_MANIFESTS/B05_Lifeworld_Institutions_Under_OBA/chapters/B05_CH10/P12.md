# B05.CH10.P12 — Failure modes and hard stops

This paragraph specifies the primary failure modes for the digital lifeworld under OBA and the hard stops that apply when systems
drift into surveillance rent, vendor lock-in, opaque block power, and escalating platform tolls. Because digital infrastructure is now
essentials infrastructure, these failure modes translate directly into L escalation (affordability, lockout clamp, cognitive harm),
extraction (E) dominance (take-rates, fee-stacks, data rent), and political/administrative block power (identity/payment exclusion).
OBA therefore defines explicit non-conformance signals and fail-closed responses. The objective is to preserve **order compatibility**:
baseline services must function without surveillance dependency; portability and interoperability must remain real; block power must be
due-process constrained; and when contestability cannot be restored, unbundling and public options become mandatory.

---

<a id="b05ch10p12s01"></a>
## B05.CH10.P12.S01 — Surveillance drift: expansion of data collection “for safety” → non-conform without necessity and sunset

A predictable failure mode is “safety” used to justify expanding data collection and profiling until surveillance rent becomes the
operating model.

### Non-conformance signals (binding)
- Expansion of data categories, retention periods, cross-service joins, or tracking surfaces without demonstrated necessity.
- Safety features that require broad behavioural profiling or third-party sharing as a default condition of baseline access.
- “Temporary” safety expansions that persist without sunset, review, and measurable benefit evidence.

### Hard stop response (binding)
- Treat expansions as non-conforming unless they satisfy: necessity proof, minimisation design, explicit sunset, and independent audit.
- Suspend non-essential data collection by default; baseline service must continue without profiling dependency (fail-closed against
  safety-washing into surveillance rent).

<a id="b05ch10p12s02"></a>
## B05.CH10.P12.S02 — Vendor capture: procurement that normalises lock-in → governance breach

Vendor capture is a primary infrastructure failure mode: procurement decisions embed proprietary stacks that block exit and
contestability.

### Capture indicators (binding)
- Proprietary formats/APIs, paywalled interoperability, exclusive app distribution, exclusive maintenance rights, and long-term
  contracts without exit tooling.
- Repeat renewals justified by “no alternative” despite lack of open standards migration plans.
- Critical data and operational control held hostage by vendors under “commercial confidentiality”.

### Hard stop response (binding)
- Treat capture as a governance breach: suspend procurement stream, require open standards compliance, impose escrow/continuity
  requirements, and trigger audit-of-the-auditor on procurement decisions.
- Where lock-in is embedded, mandate interface opening and staged migration; repeat capture triggers governance reset (fail-closed
  against procurement lock-in).

<a id="b05ch10p12s03"></a>
## B05.CH10.P12.S03 — Shadow banning/opaque ranking: block power without audit → hard stop and remedies

Opaque ranking and shadow suppression are block power mechanisms: they control reach, income, and participation without due process.

### Non-conformance signals (binding)
- Visibility suppression without disclosure, reason codes, and appeal; inconsistent enforcement across users without auditability.
- Paid influence (payola) embedded in ranking without labeling and separation.
- Refusal to allow authorised audits of rule application, feature classes, and enforcement logs.

### Hard stop response (binding)
- Treat the ranking/moderation system as non-conforming: require transparency minima, auditable reason codes, and appeal rails.
- If opacity persists, enforce structural remedies: interoperability mandates, interface opening, unbundling of ranking from monetised
  promotion layers, and public option deployment for essential functions (fail-closed against opaque block power).

<a id="b05ch10p12s04"></a>
## B05.CH10.P12.S04 — Fail-closed: if portability is lost, take-rates grow, or identity lockouts rise, public option/unbundling becomes mandatory

OBA treats deterioration of contestability indicators as evidence that the system is drifting toward incompatibility.

### Fail-closed triggers (binding)
- **Portability is lost**: portability success rates fall, switching times rise, proprietary format shares increase, or interoperability
  becomes paywalled.
- **Take-rates grow**: effective take-rates and fee-stack depth rise persistently in dominant platforms without service improvement.
- **Identity lockouts rise**: increasing lockout incident volumes and recovery times, especially where identity is needed for
  essentials access.
- **Block power expands**: deplatforming/API/payment denial without due-process constraints increases.

### Mandatory response (binding)
- Treat the current governance regime as non-conforming; initiate structural remedy selection:
  - enforce interoperability mandates and exit tooling,
  - cap take-rates and remove fee-stacks,
  - constrain lockouts with due process and emergency baseline access,
  - and where contestability cannot be restored, deploy a **public option** and/or enforce **unbundling** of dominant stacks.

### Publication requirement (binding)
- Fail-closed events, structural remedies, and conformance actions must be logged in a public conformance register (privacy-safe
  aggregation) to prevent quiet drift and selective enforcement.
