# B05.CH12 — Local institutions and governance: municipalities, executors, quasi-public bodies, contestability, and audit

This chapter specifies **local institutions** under OBA as the execution layer where policy becomes lifeworld reality. Municipalities,
regions, executing agencies, quasi-public bodies, and local commons operators hold practical block power: they allocate essentials
access (housing, care, mobility), run service desks, apply enforcement, award contracts, and decide exceptions. Without rails, local
discretion drifts into arbitrariness, postcode lotteries, vendor capture, and bureaucratic tolls (administrative L). Under OBA, local
governance is therefore treated as infrastructure: execution capacity is measurable (ΔG of governance), decision rules are
standardised where necessary, contestability (objection/appeal) is designed-in, procurement is anti-lock-in, and oversight is
multi-layered (“audit of the auditor”). Local variation is allowed only through explicit parameters; invariants remain binding.

CH12 is designed to be **anti-washing** and **fail-closed**. “Local tailoring” without written rules and audit packs is treated as
discretion creep and capture risk. Quasi-public bodies are tolerated only under strict transparency and contestability constraints,
because they are natural toll-risk structures. Emergency regimes are permitted only with sunsets and post-mortem audits to prevent
permanent power expansion. Where contestability, auditability, or procurement integrity fail persistently, OBA requires escalation that
can temporarily restrict local autonomy and force redesign.

## How to use this chapter

- If you are **designing local governance modules**: use CH12 to define roles, separation of functions, delegation boundaries
  (parameters vs invariants), and intergovernmental escalation paths.
- If you are **running local commons**: use CH12 to implement commons registries (commons_id, access rules, maintenance budgets,
  incident logs), prioritise L hotspots and R/B tails, and prevent capture through rotation and vendor-dominance checks.
- If you are **building execution capacity**: use CH12 to measure lead times/backlogs/error and drop-off, standardise templates and
  decision rules, and apply maintenance-first training/tooling continuity.
- If you are **designing dispute/appeal**: use CH12 to implement low-friction, time-bounded contestability and an independent ombuds
  function that detects systemic patterns.
- If you are **procuring locally**: use CH12 to enforce open standards, transparency logs, performance contracting, anti-corruption
  forensics, and exit assistance to prevent vendor lock-in.
- If you are **auditing**: use CH12’s audit-of-the-auditor architecture, signed releases of templates/parameters, and publication
  requirements to keep local power contestable.

## Status

This chapter is **normative** for any OBA-compatible design of local execution institutions, quasi-public bodies, local commons
portfolios, procurement regimes, and dispute/audit architectures. In particular, the separation-of-functions model, parameter/invariant
discipline, commons registry and maintenance-first rails, contestability design, audit-of-the-auditor oversight, open-standards
procurement, privacy-minimal execution data, and crisis sunset discipline are treated as **constitutive** safeguards against local
capture and administrative L.

If CH12 constraints are weakened—by discretionary deals without audit packs, opaque exceptions, procurement capture, data maximalism as
a substitute for competence, or emergency regimes without sunsets—the lifeworld layer will register predictable failure signals:
worsening lead-time tails and drop-off, higher appeal/complaint volumes, rising vendor dependency, postcode lottery patterns, and
declining legitimacy. In that case, the implementation must be treated as drifting toward incompatibility and must trigger escalation,
governance resets, procurement and template redesign, and (where necessary) temporary restriction of local autonomy until rails are
restored.
