# B05.CH09.P02 — Diagnostics: where healthcare/education becomes L (and where E grows)

This paragraph specifies the diagnostic model for healthcare and education under OBA: where these domains become major essentials
burdens (L), and where extraction (E) tends to grow. Healthcare and education become L when scarcity (staffing, facilities, training
capacity) produces long lead-times, rationing by bureaucracy, and regional mismatch; when gatekeeping networks and administrative
requirements function as clamp; and when households face indirect costs (time, paperwork, travel) that collapse R/B tails. E grows
when intermediaries and management layers become mandatory, when billing/claims complexity becomes an industry, when “compliance” is
outsourced into consulting fee-stacks, and when digital systems impose lock-in, paywalls, or opaque algorithms that cannot be audited.
The diagnostic purpose is operational: it defines mechanism families that later paragraphs must neutralise via staffing/maintenance
capacity, wait-time rails, anti-toll simplification, procurement and interoperability discipline, and fail-closed responses.

---

<a id="b05ch09p02s01"></a>
## B05.CH09.P02.S01 — Scarcity: staff, facilities, training capacity, regional mismatch

Scarcity is the primary driver of waiting and rationing, and therefore of hidden L through time costs and foregone outcomes.

### Scarcity classes (binding)
- **Staff scarcity**: insufficient clinicians/teachers/care workers; poor skill mix; inadequate supervision coverage; burnout-driven
  attrition.
- **Facility scarcity**: insufficient beds/classrooms, constrained clinic capacity, outdated or failing buildings, equipment shortages.
- **Training pipeline scarcity**: limited intake slots, placement capacity bottlenecks, slow credential pathways, and weak upskilling
  routes.
- **Regional mismatch**: capacity concentrated in high-demand areas while rural or peripheral regions face persistent gaps and travel
  burdens.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Rising wait-time distributions (median/p90/p95), growing backlogs, high staff vacancy rates, high overtime and sick leave, increasing
  turnover, and repeated temporary-staff reliance.
- Persistent regional disparities in access and outcomes that correlate with capacity gaps rather than with need.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Where scarcity persists without a measurable pipeline expansion and retention plan, the system is in capacity breach and must trigger
  staffing and maintenance-first escalation.

## B05.CH09.P02.S02 — Gatekeeping and clamp: referrals, insurance conditions, contract networks, administrative barriers

Gatekeeping becomes clamp when access depends on opaque pathways, restrictive networks, and administrative hurdles that function as
rationing by bureaucracy.

### Clamp mechanisms (binding)
- **Referral gatekeeping**: complex referral chains, discretionary triage, repeated re-referrals, and unclear criteria that delay
  access.
- **Network clamp**: insurer/commissioner contract networks that restrict provider choice, create long travel distances, or force
  repeated switching and re-documentation.
- **Administrative barriers**: documentation loops, prior authorisations, income proofs, and form burdens that primarily shift work
  onto users and frontline professionals.
- **Conditional access**: requirements to accept unrelated services, subscriptions, or monitoring to obtain essentials access.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- High denial/deferral rates with unclear reason codes; high administrative time burden; repeated “incomplete documentation” loops; and
  appeals that are slow or inaccessible.
- Access outcomes correlated with navigation skill and persistence rather than with need (bureaucracy as selection).

### Requalification trigger (binding)
- Where gatekeeping mechanisms primarily ration access without improving outcomes or safety, they are treated as E-like bureaucratic
  extraction and must be redesigned.

## B05.CH09.P02.S03 — Fee-stacks: brokerage, “management layers”, billing/claims industry, compliance consulting

Fee-stacks grow where funding flows are complex and intermediaries can charge for navigating or processing complexity.

### Fee-stack surfaces (binding)
- **Brokerage/intermediation**: mandatory agents or coordinators that add limited outcome value but extract fees from access pathways.
- **Management layers**: proliferating middle management structures whose primary output is reporting/compliance volume rather than
  measurable access and quality improvements.
- **Billing/claims industry**: coding, adjudication, denial management, and revenue-cycle services that expand with complexity.
- **Compliance consulting**: outsourced compliance, accreditation, and reporting services that become permanent spend categories without
  proportional benefits.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Rising administrative spend share; growth of consulting spend and “management” headcount; increasing time spent on billing/reporting;
  and rising disputes/denials tied to documentation rather than to eligibility.

### Requalification rule (binding)
- Where intermediary layers are mandatory and not causally linked to access or quality improvements, their revenue is classified as E
  and triggers simplification and procurement intervention.

## B05.CH09.P02.S04 — Digital toll: proprietary systems, data lock-in, paywalls for interoperability, opaque algorithms

Digital systems can convert essentials into tolls by creating lock-in and charging for access to data and interoperability.

### Digital toll mechanisms (binding)
- **Proprietary EHR/LMS lock-in**: closed formats and APIs that prevent switching and force dependence on a single vendor.
- **Paywalls for interoperability**: fees for data access, integration, or export that function as tolls on coordination and continuity.
- **Opaque algorithms**: triage/placement/risk-scoring models that affect access but cannot be audited, reproduced, or contested.
- **Workflow capture**: systems that increase frontline administrative load, shift labour into data entry, and reduce time for care or
  teaching (hidden L via reduced service capacity).

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- High switching costs; long contract renewals justified by “no alternative”; high integration spend; frequent downtime and incident
  concealment; and complaints about administrative burden from frontline staff.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Where digital toll patterns are present, procurement rails and interoperability mandates must escalate; black-box decisioning is
  treated as non-conform until made auditable (fail-closed against digital capture).
