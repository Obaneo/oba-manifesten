# B05.CH13.P04 — Career status and institutions (firms, universities, professions)

This paragraph specifies how career status and institutional gatekeeping behave under OBA. Under caps, status competition can migrate
from money toward access: elite education pathways, licences, boards, professional titles, and network positions. These institutions
are high-risk block-power surfaces: they can convert status into exclusion through opaque selection, credential inflation, and
pay-to-enter pipelines. OBA therefore requires access systems to be contestable, auditable, and non-locking; it treats professional
ethics and standards as anti-toll infrastructure that blocks E layers (fee-stacks, unsafe shortcuts, manipulative IT systems); and it
defines credential inflation as a detectable failure mode via lock-in and drop-off metrics. The goal is competence recognition without
elite choke points.

---

<a id="b05ch13p04s01"></a>
## B05.CH13.P04.S01 — Risk: status shifts to selective gatekeeping (access to education, licences, boards)

Selective gatekeeping becomes the new positional scarcity when monetary dominance is capped.

### Gatekeeping arenas (binding)
- **Education access**: selective programmes, admissions pipelines, internship/placement access, and alumni networks.
- **Licensing and accreditation**: professional licences, mandatory certificates, compliance credentials.
- **Boards and governance roles**: appointments and nominations that control institutional direction and access to opportunities.
- **Recruitment filters**: “required credentials” that are not functionally necessary but serve as status markers.

### Capture mechanisms (binding)
- Opaque selection criteria; informal networks; expensive credential pathways; unpaid internships; and “club” dynamics that reproduce
  inequality without explicit monetary accumulation.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Where access becomes the scarce resource, OBA treats selection systems as governance rails requiring auditability and contestability,
  not as private prerogatives.

<a id="b05ch13p04s02"></a>
## B05.CH13.P04.S02 — Criteria: access systems must be contestable and must not introduce inherent lock-in

Access mechanisms are permitted only if they are defensible as competence/safety requirements and designed to avoid lock-in.

### Contestability minima (binding)
- Published criteria and selection rubrics where feasible; reason codes for rejection in standard categories.
- Low-friction appeal/objection procedures; independent review for contested cases.
- Transparency about quotas and pipeline stages; no “silent rejection” and no indefinite waiting without status updates.

### No inherent lock-in (binding)
- Avoid pathways that require irreversible commitments (debt clamp, unpaid labour, relocation without support) to be eligible.
- Provide portability of credentials and recognition across institutions where feasible; avoid proprietary “only our badge counts”
  patterns.
- Where fees exist, apply anti-regression and baseline access protections; do not price-gate essential training.

### Conformance requirement (binding)
- Institutions must publish aggregated admissions and progression distributions and drop-off causes (privacy-safe aggregation).

<a id="b05ch13p04s03"></a>
## B05.CH13.P04.S03 — Professional ethics as anti-toll: quality standards that block E-layers (e.g., care, construction, IT)

Professional ethics and standards are treated as anti-extraction infrastructure when they prevent unsafe shortcuts and rent layers.

### Anti-toll function (binding)
- Standards must protect users from:
  - fee-stacks and “necessary intermediaries” that add no outcome value,
  - unsafe or fraudulent work practices,
  - opaque digital systems that create lock-in and surveillance rent (link CH09/CH10).
- Standards must be enforceable through audits and dispute processes; “ethics codes” without enforcement are theatre.

### Governance requirements (binding)
- Professional bodies must be contestable: transparent criteria, rotation, and audit-of-the-auditor; avoid closed guild dynamics.
- Enforcement must not be selectively applied to protect incumbents; selective enforcement is a hard stop.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Where professional standards are used to preserve monopoly rents rather than to protect quality and safety, they are requalified as
  E-like gatekeeping and must be redesigned.

<a id="b05ch13p04s04"></a>
## B05.CH13.P04.S04 — Failure mode: credential inflation as a new status barrier → detect via lock-in and drop-off metrics

Credential inflation is when more credentials are required without proportional competence or safety justification; it becomes a new
status barrier and a lock-in engine.

### Detection indicators (binding)
- Rising “required credential” lists for roles without changed competence requirements.
- Growth in time-to-entry and cost-to-entry for professions; increased unpaid pipeline stages.
- Increased drop-off and abandonment in training and licensing pathways (especially among lower-income cohorts).
- Increased gatekeeping complaints and appeal volumes; rising rejection rates without explained cohort-need differences.

### Fail-closed response (binding)
- Require justification audits: show the functional necessity of credentials; remove or simplify requirements where not necessary.
- Increase portability and modular recognition; create commons/public pathway options for essential training where private gatekeeping
  dominates.
- If credential inflation persists, treat the access system as non-conforming and mandate redesign with contestability and anti-lock-in
  rails (fail-closed against new elite gatekeeping).
