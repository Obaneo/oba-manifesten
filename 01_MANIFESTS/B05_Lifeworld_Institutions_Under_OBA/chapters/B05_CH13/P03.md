# B05.CH13.P03 — Consumption and “luxury” without moralising politics

This paragraph specifies how consumption norms and “luxury” behave under OBA without moralising politics. OBA does not aim to police
taste or “re-educate citizens”. The objective is structural: reduce essentials burden (L) and lock-in, and prevent consumption and
marketing systems from becoming extraction (E) engines that simulate scarcity, manufacture status anxiety, and impose cognitive toll.
Luxury persists under caps within I-MAX, but monetary display loses dominance as the primary ranking axis; other status channels become
more salient. OBA therefore focuses on governance rails—transparency, anti-manipulation, and public options—rather than moral judgments.
Where marketing and consumption systems function as coercive or deceptive tolls, they are treated as non-conforming.

---

<a id="b05ch13p03s01"></a>
## B05.CH13.P03.S01 — Goal: L down and lock-in down, not “re-educating citizens”

OBA’s target is lifeworld incidence, not virtue.

### Goal claims (binding)
- **L down**: ensure essentials affordability and reduce indirect burdens (time, friction) that force households into “status spending”
  to compensate for insecurity.
- **Lock-in down**: prevent contracts, subscriptions, and credit-driven consumption from trapping households (link to CH06/CH10 where
  relevant).
- **Agency preserved**: citizens choose within the rails; the system does not dictate preferences.

### Prohibited drift (binding)
- Cultural policy framed as moral correction (“good” vs “bad” lifestyles) is non-conforming unless it is explicitly tied to concrete
  harms (fraud, deception, coercion) and implemented through procedural rails rather than ideology.

---

<a id="b05ch13p03s02"></a>
## B05.CH13.P03.S02 — Luxury persists within I-MAX, but loses dominance as the main social ranking axis

Caps constrain the scale of monetary signalling but do not eliminate luxury consumption.

### Structural effect (binding)
- Luxury goods and experiences remain admissible within income caps; OBA does not ban luxury as a category.
- However, luxury loses dominance as a status axis because:
  - marginal spending has less positional payoff under caps,
  - commons expansion reduces “forced private substitutes” for essentials,
  - and status competition shifts toward competence, contribution, and reputation (see P02).

### Risk (binding)
- Luxury can be replaced by other scarcity signals (exclusive access, credentials, clubs). This is handled by contestability and
  anti-gatekeeping rails, not by moralising against consumption.

<a id="b05ch13p03s03"></a>
## B05.CH13.P03.S03 — Marketing as an extraction mechanism: dark patterns, scarcity simulation, reputation pressure (E in culture)

Marketing becomes extraction when it manufactures demand through manipulation and coercive status pressure.

### Extraction mechanisms (binding)
- **Dark patterns**: cancellation traps, coerced consent, hidden fees, misleading discounts, and “limited time” deception.
- **Scarcity simulation**: artificial scarcity, drop culture, and queue manipulation designed to amplify status anxiety and willingness
  to pay.
- **Reputation pressure**: targeted ads and influencer ecosystems that weaponise social comparison and fear of exclusion.
- **Lock-in design**: subscriptions, loyalty programmes, and tied ecosystems that create switching costs and data tolls.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- When marketing relies on deception or manipulation rather than on product value and transparent pricing, the activity is classified as
  E and triggers anti-manipulation enforcement.

<a id="b05ch13p03s04"></a>
## B05.CH13.P03.S04 — Remedies: transparency rails, anti-manipulation, public options; no taste police

OBA remedies target mechanisms, not preferences.

### Remedy classes (binding)
- **Transparency rails**: mandatory all-in pricing disclosure, standard contract formats, and prominent unit-cost comparisons where
  applicable; ban hidden fees and misleading reference pricing.
- **Anti-manipulation**: prohibit defined dark patterns; require opt-out symmetry; enforce clear consent flows; require reason codes for
  differential treatment (no “shadow pricing”).
- **Public options**: where consumption substitutes for essential access due to market failure (e.g., paywalled digital access,
  predatory credit, private substitutes for public services), build commons alternatives that remove coercive demand.

### No taste police (binding)
- Policy must not target lawful preferences or symbolic consumption per se. Enforcement triggers must be procedural (deception,
  coercion, opacity, lock-in), measurable, and contestable.

### Fail-closed rule (binding)
- If a consumer interface cannot provide auditable pricing, cancellation, and portability—especially for widely used subscription and
  financing products—the product is treated as non-conforming until redesigned (fail-closed against manipulation-as-default).
