# B05.CH13.P02 — Status mechanisms under caps: what changes structurally

This paragraph specifies how status mechanisms change structurally under caps (W/I) in OBA. Caps constrain monetary winner-take-all,
but they do not remove status drive. Status competition therefore shifts: it will seek alternative scarce resources and signalling
channels. This can be stabilising if status becomes attached to competence and contribution (P/ΔG), but it can also recreate extraction
(E) and block power through non-monetary capture arenas—credentials, titles, networks, platform attention, and cultural curation.
OBA therefore treats caps as necessary but not sufficient: norm and institutional design must anticipate status migration, detect new
gatekeeping surfaces, and apply contestability and audit rails so that “new elites” do not replace old rentier hierarchies.

---

<a id="b05ch13p02s01"></a>
## B05.CH13.P02.S01 — Caps limit monetary winner-take-all, but status drive remains (shift risk)

Caps change payoffs, not psychology. Status remains a scarce positional resource.

### Structural effects of caps (binding)
- Monetary accumulation beyond W/I caps loses marginal utility as a status signal; conspicuous wealth has diminishing differentiating
  power.
- Traditional “richest wins” hierarchies weaken, but competitive energy reallocates toward other scarcity dimensions: recognition,
  access, influence, and symbolic authority.

### Shift risks (binding)
- Status may migrate into:
  - credential gates (who is “qualified”),
  - network access (who is “in the room”),
  - platform attention (who is “visible”),
  - institutional control (who controls curation, awards, and norms).
- These migrations can recreate lock-in (L) and block power even if monetary inequality is reduced.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Any caps regime must be paired with anti-gatekeeping and contestability design; otherwise caps can produce cosmetic equality alongside
  new non-monetary hierarchies.

<a id="b05ch13p02s02"></a>
## B05.CH13.P02.S02 — Likely shift: status toward (i) competence, (ii) contribution/maintenance, (iii) reputational capital

OBA’s cultural objective is not to abolish status, but to align it with order-compatible outputs.

### Desired status attachment (binding direction)
- **Competence**: prestige for demonstrable skill, reliability, craftsmanship, and professional integrity.
- **Contribution/maintenance**: prestige for maintaining commons infrastructure, reducing backlog, and sustaining continuity (ΔG).
- **Reputational capital**: trust earned through transparent work, verifiable contributions, and accountable behaviour.

### Guardrails (binding)
- Competence and contribution signals must be:
  - **procedurally testable** (clear criteria, reproducible evidence),
  - **contestable** (appeal/objection paths),
  - and **non-exclusionary by default** (do not require expensive gatekeeping to demonstrate).

### Failure risk (binding)
- Reputational capital can itself become a gatekeeping asset if controlled by closed institutions or platforms; therefore portability and
  transparency requirements apply (links to CH10 platform rails).

<a id="b05ch13p02s03"></a>
## B05.CH13.P02.S03 — New arenas: certificates, titles, network access, cultural curation (non-monetary capture)

Under caps, non-monetary scarcity can become the primary status lever. These arenas are high-risk for covert elite formation.

### High-risk arenas (binding)
- **Certificates and titles**: professional badges, micro-credentials, accreditation systems, and “elite programme” labels.
- **Network access**: invitation-only conferences, boards, alumni networks, grant circles, and informal “who knows whom” gatekeeping.
- **Cultural curation**: control over what counts as legitimate art/knowledge, who is promoted, and which narratives dominate.
- **Platform reputation systems**: ranking, verification badges, follower graphs, and monetisation eligibility.

### Capture mechanisms (binding)
- Scarcity engineered through quotas and exclusivity; opaque selection criteria; pay-to-enter or pay-to-maintain status; and
  discretionary enforcement by curators.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- These arenas must be subject to the same rails as other block-power surfaces: transparent criteria, appeal rights, portability, and
  audit of the auditor. Otherwise they function as E-like toll positions.

<a id="b05ch13p02s04"></a>
## B05.CH13.P02.S04 — Implication: caps are necessary but not sufficient; norm and institutional design must detect status-shifts

Caps reduce one dimension of domination but do not guarantee order compatibility. Monitoring status migration is therefore required.

### Design implication (binding)
- Pair caps with:
  - contestable access systems (education/licensing/boards),
  - public options and commons infrastructure that reduce dependence on gatekeepers,
  - platform governance that constrains ranking and deplatforming block power,
  - and publication requirements that reveal new bottlenecks (credential inflation, exclusion, drop-off).

### Detection requirement (binding)
- Maintain indicators for:
  - credential barriers and “required badges” growth,
  - gatekeeping complaints and appeal rates,
  - access drop-off due to friction,
  - concentration in curatorial institutions,
  - and platform status capture (take-rates, ranking appeals, lockouts) (link P12 measurement discipline).

### Fail-closed rule (binding)
- If status shifts create new lock-in or block power, the relevant institution/rule must be redesigned: open criteria, portability, and
  public option/interoperability remedies where contestability cannot be restored (fail-closed against non-monetary elite capture).
