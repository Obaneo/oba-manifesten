# B05.CH07 — Housing as an essential: rent/ownership, build capacity, lock-in, commons options, and L-reduction

This chapter specifies housing as the largest **essentials (L) domain** and therefore as a primary lifeworld interface where clamp
dynamics, scarcity, and block power concentrate. Under OBA, housing policy is admissible only insofar as it reduces L through **build
and maintenance capacity (ΔG)**, contestability, contract discipline, and commons options—not through demand stimulus, credit-driven
asset inflation, or protected rentier positions. When housing becomes a toll system (rent as a scarcity rent), a fee-stack ecosystem
(brokerage/management/service charges), or a lock-in regime (exit penalties, blacklisting, opaque allocation), it is requalified as E
and must be neutralised.

CH07 is designed to be **anti-washing** and **fail-closed**. Programmes that stabilise or raise prices by expanding purchasing power,
subsidising demand, or loosening credit are treated as **prohibited channels** (ΔM leakage into assets). Likewise, policies that
normalise lock-in, conceal scarcity by process friction, or tolerate maintenance backlogs are treated as non-conforming until
redesigned. The chapter therefore combines diagnostics, rails, templates, metrics, publication discipline, and hard stops so the
housing layer remains contestable, auditable, and non-captured.

## How to use this chapter

- If you are **designing housing policy**: use CH07 to choose supply/maintenance-first instruments, reject demand-stimulus schemes
  that inflate prices, and bind interventions to measurable L reduction (cost burden tails, wait times, backlog indicators).
- If you are **designing land and permitting regimes**: use CH07 to treat land scarcity, permit friction, and infrastructure gating as
  hidden L; apply standardisation, time norms, decision logs, and anti-corruption rails.
- If you are **designing rental regulation**: use CH07 to implement rent rails, standard lease templates, prohibited clamp clauses,
  look-through on landlord structures, and fast dispute procedures with reproducible calculations.
- If you are **designing ownership finance**: use CH07 to avoid credit-driven asset inflation, enforce bounded LTV and transparent
  all-in pricing, and prefer non-speculative shared-equity or commons-linked alternatives with capped upside.
- If you are **building housing commons**: use CH07 to define commons types, governance minima, financing channels (ΔM only into
  commons capex and ring-fenced maintenance), and anti-commons-washing criteria.
- If you are **auditing lifeworld outcomes**: use P12 and P10 to track housing cost burdens, vacancy and wait-time metrics,
  eviction/foreclosure rates, maintenance backlogs, incident statistics, and clamp indicators (exit friction, penalties, blacklisting).

## Status

This chapter is **normative** for any OBA-compatible design of housing markets, land regimes, rental contracts, ownership finance, and
housing commons. In particular, the admissibility rails (no demand-stimulus asset-support), build/maintenance-first priority,
contract and rent discipline, look-through on landlord structures, commons governance minima, clamp detection metrics, and publication
requirements are treated as **constitutive** safeguards against housing-driven L escalation.

If CH07 constraints are weakened—through credit easing that inflates prices, tolerated permit bottlenecks, capture by construction/land
lobbies, opaque landlord structures, or maintenance neglect—the lifeworld layer will register predictable failure signals: rising
housing cost burdens (especially tails), longer wait times, increased eviction/foreclosure rates, growing backlogs, and worsening
lock-in metrics. In that case, the implementation must be treated as drifting toward incompatibility and must trigger redesign,
scope restriction to auditable coverage, and (where necessary) governance resets or hard stops on prohibited channels.
