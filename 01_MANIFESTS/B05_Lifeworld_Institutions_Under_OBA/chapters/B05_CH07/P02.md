# B05.CH07.P02 — Diagnostics: why housing becomes L (and when it becomes E)

This paragraph specifies the diagnostic model for housing under OBA: why housing becomes a dominant essentials burden (L), and under
which conditions housing arrangements and revenues must be requalified as extraction (E). Housing becomes L when scarcity, process
friction, and power asymmetry push costs and clamp upward faster than incomes and commons substitution can compensate. Housing becomes
E when actors can sustain toll positions—through land/permit bottlenecks, portfolio concentration, fee-stacks, and contract lock-in—
such that payments primarily fund scarcity rents and intermediated extraction rather than productive housing capacity or maintenance.
The diagnostic purpose is operational: it defines the mechanisms that later paragraphs must neutralise via supply/maintenance-first
capacity, contestability rails, and fail-closed prohibitions on price-support channels.

---

## B05.CH07.P02.S01 — Scarcity and bottlenecks: land, permits, build capacity, infrastructure, materials

Under OBA, housing scarcity is treated as a multi-stage pipeline problem. Bottlenecks convert demand into price pressure and waiting
time, increasing L even when nominal “policy spending” rises.

### Bottleneck classes (binding)
- **Land and location**: constrained serviced land, speculative withholding, fragmented ownership, and inability to assemble plots.
- **Permits and process**: long approval cycles, discretionary variances, opaque objections, and administrative backlog that function as
  hidden L through time costs and reduced throughput.
- **Build capacity**: shortages of skilled labour, limited contractor competition, thin supply chains, and low productivity modes that
  constrain output.
- **Infrastructure gating**: insufficient utilities, transport, schools/health access, and network capacity that prevent build-out in
  otherwise available areas.
- **Materials and standards**: commodity volatility, supply shocks, and lack of standardisation/prefab pathways that raise cost per
  unit and increase cycle time.

### Diagnostic rule (binding)
- If housing costs rise while any bottleneck class shows worsening throughput (cycle time, approvals, capacity utilisation, backlog),
  the problem is structural scarcity. Demand-side relief in this condition is presumed to inflate prices (non-conform risk).

## B05.CH07.P02.S02 — Rentier mechanism: rent as a toll under structural scarcity and block power

Rent becomes a toll when scarcity is persistent and landlords (or intermediaries controlling access) have block power over essentials.

### Mechanism (binding)
- **Structural scarcity** creates inelastic demand: households must pay or lose access to life participation.
- **Block power** (concentration, control of allocation channels, information advantage) allows rent to rise above functional costs.
- **Lock-in** (moving friction, deposits, blacklisting) suppresses contestability, enabling sustained extraction.

### Requalification criteria (binding)
- Rent and housing-related revenues are requalified as **E** to the extent they reflect:
  - scarcity rents (price above competitive/functional baseline in persistent shortage),
  - monopoly/oligopoly pricing enabled by concentration or coordinated behaviour,
  - exploitation of allocation control (preferred access, gatekeeping fees, discriminatory steering).

### Diagnostic indicator set (binding)
- Rising rent burden tails (p90/p95), low vacancy with high churn costs, growth of portfolio landlord share, and persistent divergence
  between rent increases and maintenance/service improvements indicate toll formation.

## B05.CH07.P02.S03 — Fee-stacks: management, brokerage, service charges, financing layers as E cascades

Housing markets generate fee-stacks when intermediaries can insert charges into unavoidable transactions (moving, leasing, repairs,
financing).

### Fee-stack surfaces (binding)
- **Management layers**: multiple servicing entities each charging “standard” fees without demonstrable additional value.
- **Brokerage and listing tolls**: compulsory intermediaries in allocation, renewals, and moving, especially where supply is scarce.
- **Service charges**: opaque utilities/maintenance surcharges, bundled building fees, and markups through captive providers.
- **Financing layers**: origination, refinancing friction, add-ons, and “insurance” bundles that function as margin padding.

### Requalification rule (binding)
- Any charge whose functional effect is not a bounded productive cost (P) of building/maintenance/operations is classified as **E**.
  Labels (“service”, “administration”, “premium”) do not control classification.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Increasing fee share of total housing cost, unexplained variance across similar units, complex invoices, affiliate self-dealing, and
  high dispute volumes over charges indicate fee-stack regrowth.

## B05.CH07.P02.S04 — Contract clamp: long terms, penalties, income requirements, blacklisting, information asymmetry

Housing becomes clamp when contracts and allocation systems convert scarcity into lock-in and coercion.

### Clamp mechanisms (binding)
- **Long or rigid terms** that make exit economically punitive (high notice periods, renewal traps, non-portable deposits).
- **Penalty structures**: break fees, “restoration” charges, and discretionary damage claims that function as leverage.
- **Income/guarantor requirements** that exceed realistic affordability logic and become gatekeeping tools under shortage.
- **Blacklisting and exclusion**: opaque registries, informal “bad tenant” systems, and automated denial without dispute rights.
- **Information asymmetry**: non-standard contracts, unclear obligations, hidden defects, and lack of comparable price/service terms.

### Diagnostic rule (binding)
- If moving becomes financially infeasible for typical households (exit friction rises), or if denial/blacklisting occurs without
  transparent rules and objection flow, housing governance is drifting into clamp and must trigger contestability and contract-rail
  interventions.
