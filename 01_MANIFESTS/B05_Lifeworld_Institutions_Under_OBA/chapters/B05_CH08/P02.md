# B05.CH08.P02 — Diagnostics: where energy/mobility becomes L (and where E grows)

This paragraph specifies the diagnostic model for energy and mobility under OBA: where these domains become major essentials burdens
(L), and where extraction (E) tends to grow. Energy and mobility become L when reliability falls, tariffs become regressive or opaque,
and households are forced into lock-in structures (car dependence, fossil dependence, vendor-dependent service models) that increase
recurring costs and reduce resilience. E grows when natural monopoly segments (grids/rails) are converted into toll positions, when
tariff complexity becomes a fee-stack ecosystem, and when procurement and operations drift into vendor capture and financial
engineering. The diagnostic purpose is operational: it defines the mechanism families that later paragraphs must neutralise via tariff
rails, commons capacity governance (ΔG/maintenance), procurement discipline, and fail-closed responses to shocks and lock-in.

---

<a id="b05ch08p02s01"></a>
## B05.CH08.P02.S01 — Natural monopolies: grids/rails → toll risk and underinvestment risk

Grids and many mobility networks have natural monopoly characteristics: duplication is inefficient, so governance determines whether
they become reliable commons capacity or toll infrastructure.

### Toll risk (binding)
- Monopoly operators can extract via regulated margins, opaque fees, and concession indexation, turning unavoidable access into a toll.
- Where competition is simulated through complex intermediaries, extraction can shift into billing layers, switching friction, and
  “service” add-ons rather than into improved delivery.

### Underinvestment risk (binding)
- Operators can also underinvest: defer maintenance, delay upgrades, and externalise reliability risk to households and firms.
- Underinvestment manifests as rising incidents, growing maintenance backlog, declining reliability, and higher shock sensitivity.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Rising fixed charges and non-transparent surcharges; persistent reliability degradation (outages, delays); backlog growth; and
  rising operator profitability without measurable ΔG/reliability improvements.

### Requalification trigger (binding)
- If monopoly returns rise while reliability and maintenance performance deteriorate, the segment is treated as an E-toll drift and
  triggers governance and tariff intervention.

<a id="b05ch08p02s02"></a>
## B05.CH08.P02.S02 — Tariff structures: fixed charges as regressive; peak pricing; opaque surcharges as fee-stacks

Tariff design is a primary interface between infrastructure economics and lifeworld affordability. Poor tariff structure raises L even
if headline prices appear stable.

### Regressive fixed charges (binding)
- High fixed charges shift costs onto low-consumption households and make affordability less elastic; this increases L tails and
  worsens R/B for low-income cohorts.
- Fixed charges can function as an extraction surface when they are not linked to auditable fixed costs and service obligations.

### Peak pricing and volatility (binding)
- Peak pricing can reflect real system constraints, but without baseline protection it can create clamp during cold spells, heat waves,
  or commuting necessity.
- Volatility exposure becomes L when households cannot hedge or shift consumption (low flexibility, poor insulation, no mobility
  alternatives).

### Opaque surcharges as fee-stacks (binding)
- Bundled surcharges, “administration” fees, network add-ons, and complex multi-part tariffs can become a fee-stack ecosystem that
  prevents contestation and enables margin padding.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Rising share of bill attributable to fixed and opaque components; increasing dispersion across similar households; high complaint and
  dispute volumes; and tariff complexity that cannot be reproduced from disclosed parameters.

## B05.CH08.P02.S03 — Lock-in: car dependence, fossil dependence, vendor lock-in in grid operations/IT

Lock-in is a core mechanism that converts essentials into clamp: households and communities lose exit options and become price takers.

### Car dependence (binding)
- When public transport coverage is weak and land-use patterns force car ownership, mobility cost becomes a quasi-fixed tax: fuel,
  insurance, depreciation, repairs, and financing costs raise L and increase shock sensitivity.

### Fossil dependence (binding)
- Dependence on fossil fuels (heating, transport) exposes households to geopolitical price shocks and supply disruptions; it raises L
  and worsens R/B tails under volatility.

### Vendor lock-in (binding)
- Proprietary grid control systems, closed data interfaces, exclusive maintenance contracts, and non-interoperable hardware/software
  stacks create long-term dependence, raise lifecycle costs, and reduce resilience and contestability.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- High share of household budgets committed to mobility/energy essentials; low modal alternatives; inability to switch suppliers or
  technologies; long-term exclusive IT/operations contracts; and repeated emergency procurement justified by “no alternative”.

## B05.CH08.P02.S04 — Shocks: geopolitics, weather dependence, supply chains; translates into R/B tails

Energy and mobility shocks transmit directly into lifeworld tails: sudden price breaks, outages, and service disruptions collapse R/B.

### Shock classes (binding)
- **Geopolitics**: fuel supply disruptions, sanctions, pipeline constraints, shipping disruptions.
- **Weather dependence**: hydrology and wind variability, cold snaps, heat waves, storms affecting grids and transport.
- **Supply chains**: transformer shortages, battery/material constraints, semiconductor issues, rolling stock lead times, repair parts.

### Translation mechanism (binding)
- Shocks become lifeworld harm when tariffs expose households to volatility, when baseline access is not protected, and when the system
  lacks redundancy and rapid-repair capacity.
- Mobility shocks (rail failures, fuel shortages) translate into missed work/education access, increasing L indirectly through time and
  opportunity loss.

### Diagnostic indicators (binding)
- Price spikes correlated with arrears/disconnections; increased outage durations; service cancellations; and widening burden tails in
  affected regions/cohorts.

### Conformance implication (binding)
- Repeated shocks without resilience improvements indicate underinvestment and governance failure; this triggers maintenance-first
  escalation, tariff redesign, and procurement/reserve capacity interventions (fail-closed against chronic tail exposure).
