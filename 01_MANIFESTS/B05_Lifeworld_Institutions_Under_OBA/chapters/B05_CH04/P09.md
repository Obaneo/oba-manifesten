# B05.CH04.P09 — Administrative friction as hidden L (and how to reduce it)

This paragraph defines administrative friction as **hidden L**: a burdens layer created by procedures, proof regimes, and interfaces.
In security systems, administrative friction is not a side effect—it is often the primary gate. It determines whether rights are
accessible in practice, how quickly shocks are absorbed, and whether people are pushed into arrears or clamp contracts while waiting.

Under OBA, administrative friction is treated as an order variable: it must be measured, published, and reduced through
standardisation and automation **without** surveillance drift. When friction becomes chronic, it functions as a **bureaucratic toll**:
an E-like ecosystem of forms, consultants, and intermediaries grows around access. CH04 therefore requires explicit anti-toll rules:
procedures that generate friction must be redesigned or requalified as non-conforming.

---

## B05.CH04.P09.S01 — Friction types: form burden, waiting time, proof pressure, re-assessments, digital exclusion

Administrative L manifests through predictable friction types.

### Canonical friction types (minimum set)
- **Form burden**: number of forms, complexity, repeated data entry, confusing language, inconsistent requirements.
- **Waiting time**: lead times to decisions, payments, appeals, and corrections; queueing becomes time rationing.
- **Proof pressure**: excessive documentation demands, “prove hardship” loops, repeated verification, audits-as-deterrence.
- **Re-assessments**: frequent re-checks that consume time and create instability; eligibility churn as a clamp.
- **Digital exclusion**: rights require digital identity, devices, stable internet, or app ecosystems; lockouts become denial.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Security mechanisms must assume low administrative capacity under stress; procedures must work in the worst-case lifeworld state.
- Any friction type that materially increases arrears risk or delays crisis relief is treated as a design failure, not as user error.

---

## B05.CH04.P09.S02 — Standardisation: one-stop interface, uniform criteria, machine-readable decision rules

Friction reduction requires standardisation and predictable decision rules. Standardisation is not “central planning”; it is
anti-arbitrariness and anti-fee-stack design.

### Standardisation components (binding)
- **One-stop interface**: single entrypoint for applications, status, and appeals; reduce cross-agency shuttling.
- **Uniform criteria**: shared eligibility criteria and evidence standards; avoid postcode lotteries and contradictory requirements.
- **Machine-readable decision rules**: publish decision logic in a form that is inspectable and testable; enable reproducibility and
  appeal.
- **Pre-fill and reuse**: where data is already held lawfully for the purpose, pre-fill and avoid re-submission (purpose limitation).

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Standardisation must not become data maximalism: only order-necessary data is used; no secondary profiling.
- If rules cannot be made inspectable (black-box vendors/algorithms), the system is non-conforming.

## B05.CH04.P09.S03 — Anti-bureaucratic toll: procedures that generate friction are redesigned/requalified

When procedures systematically create friction, they function as a toll gate. OBA treats this as a governance failure mode analogous
to fee-stacks.

### Bureaucratic toll mechanisms
- Complexity that requires intermediaries (consultants, paid advisors) to access rights.
- Proof regimes designed as deterrence (“make it hard so fewer apply”).
- Discretionary gatekeeping masked as “case-by-case” necessity.
- Endless re-assessment cycles that keep households in constant uncertainty.

### Requalification rule (binding)
- Procedures that generate chronic friction must be treated as non-conforming and must be redesigned.
- If redesign is not possible within capacity/privacy constraints, the scope of the security instrument must be reduced (fail-closed)
  to what can be executed reliably and fairly.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any entity profiting from access friction (paid intermediaries) carries burden of proof that it is not operating as an E layer.
- “Anti-fraud” justification is not sufficient if fraud controls rely on generalised punishment (delays, stigma) rather than targeted,
  auditable checks.

## B05.CH04.P09.S04 — Measurability: friction proxies (lead time, drop-off, appeal rates) without personal profiling

Administrative L must be measurable without creating surveillance regimes.

### Required friction proxies (minimum; binding)
- **Lead time**: application-to-decision, decision-to-payment, appeal duration; distributions (median + tails).
- **Drop-off**: started applications not completed; eligible but not served; abandonment reasons (survey-based/aggregated).
- **Appeal rates**: appeal frequency, reversal rates, error categories, correction time.
- **Re-assessment churn**: frequency of re-checks, termination/reinstatement cycles, instability indicators.
- **Digital lockouts**: identity recovery times, failed login rates for essential portals (aggregated), assisted-access usage.

### Privacy rails (binding)
- Publish only aggregates with suppression and join-safety; no individual tracking to “improve compliance.”
- Use sampling and surveys for experience burdens; if measurement requires invasive monitoring, redesign the measurement approach.

### Conformance rule (binding)
- If friction proxies worsen (longer lead times, higher drop-off) without external shock explanation, the system must treat this as
  increased hidden L and trigger redesign and escalation.
