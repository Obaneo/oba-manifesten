# B03.CH11.P02 — Conflict sources: structural tensions in OBA governance

This paragraph specifies the **structural sources** of internal conflict in OBA governance. These are not accidental disputes but predictable pressure
zones created by OBA rails: binding caps constrain extraction, ΔM becomes contestable allocation, commons are prioritised and therefore rationed under
capacity limits, and auditability forces disagreements into definition and measurement space. The function of this paragraph is diagnostic: to identify
where conflict will emerge, what signatures it has, and which governance interfaces must be pre-built to prevent drift into sabotage, capture, or
authoritarian shortcuts.

## Core principles

* **Conflict sources are rail-adjacent**: the closer to caps/ΔM/commons/auditability, the more intense and strategic the conflict.
* **Scarcity and timing are accelerants**: even correct rails generate backlash when burdens are concentrated or benefits delayed.
* **Measurement is a battlefield**: when politics is forced into evidence, actors fight over definitions and uncertainty.
* **Pre-commitment reduces violence**: explicit criteria, registries, and appeals convert power struggles into governable procedures.

## B03.CH11.P02.S01 — ΔM allocation: distributive conflict over investments and priorities

ΔM allocation makes distributive politics explicit. Under OBA, monetary/fiscal channels are not “neutral background”; they are deliberate instruments
with measurable routing rules. That transparency generates conflict because allocation choices create visible winners and losers.

### ΔM conflict mechanisms (canonical)

* **Priority disputes**: competing claims on investment (housing vs energy vs health vs infrastructure), often under constrained budgets.
* **Geographic distribution**: regions perceive unfairness when allocation concentrates in growth hubs or politically central areas.
* **Sectoral conflict**: productive sectors demand investment; rent-seeking sectors demand protection; both frame as “national interest.”
* **Instrument conflict**: grants vs guarantees vs procurement vs tax credits; each has different capture and audit profiles.
* **Allocation opacity pressure**: actors push for discretion (“case-by-case”) to enable side-deals and exception trading.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* ΔM allocation **MUST** be governed by published criteria and logged decisions; discretionary “strategic exceptions” are capture surfaces.
* High-impact allocations **MUST** be appealable and must publish mechanism and axis impacts (E/RB/caps/ΔG/ΔM).
* If allocation disputes correlate with rising exceptions or vendor lock-in, escalation must treat it as capture-risk, not mere politics.

## B03.CH11.P02.S02 — Commons access: scarcity, prioritisation, boundary-rule interpretation

Commons create new rights and expectations: open access within boundary rules. Conflict arises when capacity is limited, when quality varies, or when
boundary rules are contested. Commons politics becomes intense because it touches daily life (essentials) and because it replaces price rationing with
rule-based rationing.

### Commons conflict mechanisms (canonical)

* **Scarcity conflicts**: demand exceeds capacity (housing, care, energy grid capacity, education slots); prioritisation becomes distributive politics.
* **Boundary-rule disputes**: what counts as eligible use, what is abuse, what is “free riding,” and how rules apply to edge cases.
* **Gating attempts**: operators or organised groups attempt to restrict access (formal or informal), recreating toll-like privileges.
* **Quality disputes**: uneven service quality produces legitimacy conflict (“commons promised, but reality is degraded”).
* **Maintenance tension (ΔG)**: investment in maintenance competes with expansion; neglect creates crises and scapegoating.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Commons **MUST** publish boundary rules, prioritisation criteria, and complaint/appeal routes; informal rationing is non-compliant.
* Capacity shortages **MUST** trigger surge planning and ΔG repair protocols, not ad hoc gatekeeping.
* If access disputes produce profiling/surveillance proposals, internal safety rails (P07) must be activated.

## B03.CH11.P02.S03 — Caps: exception pressure, near-cap clustering, and elite splitting

Caps bind top-tail extraction and reduce the space for toll positions. Conflict concentrates here because caps directly constrain powerful actors’ payoff
structures. The predictable result is continuous **exception pressure**, strategic compliance engineering, and elite splits between actors who can live
inside the rails and those whose model depends on bypass.

### Cap conflict mechanisms (canonical)

* **Exception pressure**: permanent lobbying for carve-outs, special regimes, transitional waivers, and “strategic sector” status.
* **Near-cap clustering**: actors optimise to sit just below caps through fragmentation, reclassification, netting, and timing games.
* **Elite splitting**: productive capital may accept caps as anti-monopoly infrastructure; finance/toll actors resist and fund backlash.
* **Enforcement wars**: conflicts move to audit scope, definitions, and sanction discretion (“too harsh,” “uncompetitive,” “capital flight”).
* **Crisis exploitation**: shocks are used to suspend caps (“temporary”) and normalise exceptions.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Exception registries and renewal discipline **MUST** be strict; routine renewals are regression signals.
* Near-cap clustering **MUST** be monitored and countered with anti-gaming rules (equivalence doctrines, consolidation tests).
* If sanction rates fall while near-cap clustering rises, escalation must treat it as capture or enforcement compromise.

## B03.CH11.P02.S04 — R/B: timing asymmetry, local burden concentration, swing backlash

R/B (restruimte and burden distribution) is where political stability is won or lost. Even if OBA is structurally superior, concentrated short-run
burdens can create swing backlash that threatens the entire project. The conflict is often not about the rails themselves, but about **sequencing** and
**buffers**.

### R/B conflict mechanisms (canonical)

* **Timing asymmetry**: benefits (lower extraction, better commons) arrive later; costs (reallocation, compliance, transition) arrive now.
* **Local concentration**: specific regions or sectors bear disproportionate adjustment costs (industrial decline, housing price resets, pension changes).
* **Status-loss anxiety**: groups perceive relative loss even if absolute welfare improves; narrative capture uses this as a wedge.
* **Essentials continuity risk**: if essentials delivery degrades during transition, legitimacy collapses regardless of long-run gains.
* **Compensation drift risk**: pressure for asset-support reflex (protecting incumbent owners) re-enters via “stabilisation packages.”

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* OBA implementation **MUST** include R/B lower bounds and essentials continuity constraints; stability is an order requirement.
* Buffer instruments must be designed to avoid E-preservation (no permanent toll protection); stabilisation must be time-bound and auditable.
* If swing backlash correlates with scapegoating or exception stacking, escalation must treat it as schism risk (P04 triggers).

## B03.CH11.P02.S05 — Measurement and audit layer: definition shifting, uncertainty, perceived technocracy

When politics becomes evidence-bound, conflict migrates to measurement. Actors contest definitions, uncertainty ranges, and modelling assumptions. If the
audit layer is not contestable and replicable, it is perceived as technocratic rule—fuel for legitimacy conflict and narrative capture.

### Measurement conflict mechanisms (canonical)

* **Definition shifting**: subtle semantic changes to reclassify extraction as productive or to move activity outside caps/ΔM rules.
* **Uncertainty weaponisation**: either overstating certainty to force outcomes, or overstating uncertainty to block enforcement (“can’t prove it”).
* **Model monopoly**: a single vendor or ministry model becomes authoritative; replication is blocked by IP or data access.
* **Metric substitution**: proxies replace core outcomes; dashboards become gameable and politically weaponised.
* **Audit conflict**: audits trigger disputes over sampling, thresholds, and sanction proportionality; enforcement becomes politicised.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Measurement systems **MUST** be replicable with version pinning and public changelogs; otherwise “evidence-based” becomes authority laundering.
* Definition changes **MUST** trigger restatements and bridging; un-restated changes are capture indicators.
* Where uncertainty is material, decisions **MUST** publish uncertainty bands and contingency rules; hiding uncertainty is non-compliant.
