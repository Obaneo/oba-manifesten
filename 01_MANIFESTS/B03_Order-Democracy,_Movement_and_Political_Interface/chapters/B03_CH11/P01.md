# B03.CH11.P01 — Purpose and scope: internal conflict as a systemic risk in order-democracy

This paragraph defines **internal conflict** as a **systemic risk** inside OBA-compatible order-democracy. OBA is not designed to eliminate conflict; it
is designed to **force conflict into governable form**: disputes must be expressed as parameter choices, classifications, evidence disagreements, and
procedural appeals—rather than as sabotage, factional enforcement, or semantic drift. The central claim is that OBA’s core rails (binding caps, ΔM
allocation discipline, commons priority, and auditability) make certain conflicts **inevitable** and therefore must be treated as first-class design
objects.

The chapter’s purpose is to codify how internal conflict is diagnosed, escalated, and recovered from without breaking OBA rails or regressing into OOA
reflexes during crisis windows.

## Core principles

* **Conflict is endogenous**: OBA creates predictable internal tensions by binding extraction and making allocation visible.
* **Governability is the aim**: conflict must be routable through evidence and procedure, not through power games.
* **Schism is a failure mode**: unmanaged conflict becomes fork pressure, capture opportunity, or authoritarian drift.
* **Recovery must be executable**: rollback, re-authorisation, reconstitution, and restatements must be pre-encoded.

## B03.CH11.P01.S01 — Why OBA generates internal conflict (caps, ΔM, commons priority)

OBA generates internal conflict because it replaces implicit power distributions with explicit rails. When extraction is bounded and allocation is made
auditable, groups that previously benefited from opacity lose discretionary room, while groups that depend on stability demand continuity. Conflict is
therefore not an anomaly; it is the system revealing trade-offs that were previously hidden.

### Primary endogenous conflict generators

* **Caps bind privileges**: caps constrain top-tail dominance and toll positions; this triggers exception pressure, near-cap clustering, and elite splitting.
* **ΔM becomes explicit**: allocation of money flows (investment, subsidies, guarantees, procurement) becomes visible and contestable, creating distributive conflict.
* **Commons priority creates scarcity politics**: where commons capacity is limited, access and prioritisation become contested; boundary rules become politically loaded.
* **Auditability converts narratives into disputes**: claims must be measurable; actors shift from slogan conflict to definition/measurement conflict (a higher-skill arena).
* **Timing asymmetry is exposed**: transitions impose costs unevenly over time; swing groups experience short-run losses before long-run gains materialise.

### Conformance implications

Internal conflict under OBA will concentrate around: (i) exceptions, (ii) definitions, (iii) allocation criteria, and (iv) enforcement selectivity. A
system that claims OBA but has “no internal conflict” is either not binding rails or is suppressing contestability—both are regression signals.

## B03.CH11.P01.S02 — Delimitation: this chapter covers conflict mechanics and recovery, not ideology

This chapter does not prescribe party-political positions, cultural values, or ideological identity. It specifies **mechanics**: how conflict is
structured, how it escalates, and how the system recovers while preserving rails.

### What is in scope

* Conflict typology: parametric, interpretive, institutional, legitimacy, existential rupture.
* Escalation architecture: complaint → mediation → arbitration/tribunal → judicial review, with evidence discipline.
* Hard stops: conditions under which operations must fail-closed (measurement blackout, cap erosion, black-box allocation).
* Recovery: rollback, re-authorisation, reconstitution, repair of commons capacity, restatements.

### What is out of scope

* Party endorsements, electoral strategy, or “who to vote for.”
* Ideological adjudication (“left is right” / “right is wrong”).
* Constitutional design as such (only interfaces and procedural rails, not full constitutional blueprints).

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Any use of this chapter in advocacy **MUST** preserve its procedural character; importing ideology into the protocol layer is a semantic drift vector.
* Claims of legitimacy **MUST** be argued through the defined conflict mechanisms (evidence/procedure), not through identity claims.

## B03.CH11.P01.S03 — Relation to forks/compatibility (B03.CH07) and movement (B03.CH06)

Internal conflict becomes dangerous when it is misclassified. Some conflicts are normal parameter disputes; others indicate incompatibility or capture.
This chapter connects conflict handling to two adjacent modules.

### Interface with B03.CH07 (forks and compatibility)

* When conflict attacks **non-negotiables** (caps/commons/auditability) or produces sustained black-box allocation, it becomes fork pressure.
* The **fork protocol** (classification + name label + compatibility status) is the correct route when reconciliation would require breaking rails.
* This chapter supplies the escalation and recovery machinery that should be attempted **before** a fork is declared, except in hard-stop conditions.

### Interface with B03.CH06 (movement and coalition dynamics)

* Movements are the carriers of OBA adoption; internal conflicts are also coalition conflicts (swing group stress, elite splitting, narrative warfare).
* B03.CH06 explains how coalitions form and fracture; this chapter specifies how those fractures are routed through governable procedures.
* During conflict, capture actors exploit movement fragmentation; anti-capture constraints (B03.CH10) become more critical.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Conflict handling **MUST** include a fork-recognition rule: when rails are attacked, the system must either restore rails or explicitly fork and relabel.
* Movement governance **MUST** avoid semantic blending (keeping one label across incompatible rails); name hygiene is mandatory.

## B03.CH11.P01.S04 — Output: conflict typology + escalation ladder + recovery protocols

The output of this chapter is an operational set of artefacts that make internal conflict governable and recoverable.

### Output A — Conflict typology (diagnostic map)

* **Conflict sources** (ΔM allocation, commons access, caps pressure, R/B timing, measurement/audit disputes).
* **Conflict modes** (parametric, interpretive, institutional, legitimacy, existential rupture).
* **Schism triggers** (exceptions explosion, sanction collapse, reproducibility failure, black-box allocation, scapegoating/incident politics).

### Output B — Escalation ladder (procedure + evidence)

* **Complaint → mediation → arbitration/tribunal → judicial review**, with non-suspensive defaults.
* **Evidence discipline**: logs, definition sets, audit trails, replication requirements, restatements.
* **Hard stops**: fail-closed conditions when measurability or contestability is lost.

### Output C — Recovery protocols (restore rails)

* **Rollback** of exceptions, contracts, tooling changes, and captured decisions.
* **Re-authorisation** where constitutive deviation is proposed or has occurred.
* **Reconstitution** of compromised bodies (rotation, replacement, independent review).
* **Repair** of commons capacity and neutralisation of crisis rents.
* **Restatements** to repair time series and bridge definition shifts.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Systems claiming OBA-compatible governance **MUST** implement the typology, escalation ladder, and recovery protocols as pre-committed mechanisms.
* If conflict is handled via ad hoc power, secrecy, or indefinite emergency measures, the regime **MUST** be classified as drift-risk or non-compatible.
