# B03.CH11.P03 — Conflict modes: types of internal conflict and their signatures

This paragraph defines **conflict modes** inside OBA governance: recurrent forms that internal disputes take, each with characteristic signatures and
different resolution pathways. The critical function is **classification**: misclassifying a conflict mode is how order-democracy breaks. Treating an
existential rupture as a normal parameter dispute produces drift; treating a parameter dispute as an existential threat produces authoritarian overreach.

OBA therefore distinguishes (i) parametric conflict (within rails), (ii) interpretive conflict (about classification and boundary rules), (iii)
institutional conflict (between bodies and enforcement actors), (iv) legitimacy conflict (about mandate and procedure), and (v) existential rupture
(attacks on non-negotiables). Each mode has distinct evidence requirements, escalation thresholds, and hard-stop implications.

## Core principles

* **Conflict mode determines protocol**: the same content dispute requires different procedures depending on mode.
* **Evidence discipline is the stabiliser**: modes differ in what counts as admissible evidence and how uncertainty is handled.
* **Hard stops are mode-specific**: existential rupture and black-box allocation trigger fail-closed behaviour faster than parametric disputes.
* **Name hygiene prevents drift**: when rupture implies rail deviation, fork protocol and relabeling must activate (B03.CH07).

## B03.CH11.P03.S01 — Parametric conflict: thresholds/tempo/priorities within the rails

Parametric conflict is disagreement over **settings** inside agreed rails: rates, thresholds, sequencing, budget bands, and prioritisation. It is the
normal mode of order-democracy under OBA. The rails remain intact; the dispute is about how to operate within them.

### Typical objects

* Cap levels and implementation pace (within the cap rail, not abolishing it).
* ΔM allocation bands (priority weights, sequencing, regional distribution).
* Commons capacity expansion tempo and service levels.
* Enforcement intensity parameters (sampling rate, inspection cadence), within published protocols.

### Signatures

* Parties accept the same definition set and audit regime.
* Dispute is expressible as trade-offs with measurable impacts (E/RB/ΔG/ΔM).
* Compromise is possible via calibration and sequencing.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Parametric disputes **MUST** be resolved via rule-based decision procedures (mandate parameters, councils, panels), not via exceptions.
* Decisions **MUST** publish parameter deltas, expected impacts, and review dates; otherwise “parameter choices” become stealth drift.

## B03.CH11.P03.S02 — Interpretive conflict: classification (E vs productive), boundary rules, exposure tests

Interpretive conflict is disagreement about **how to classify** objects under the rails: what counts as extraction vs productive return, who is exposed,
what is a valid commons use, and which boundary rule applies. This mode is more technical and is a prime capture surface because it operates through
definitions and edge cases.

### Typical objects

* Classification disputes: fee-stack vs service cost; toll vs productive profit; rent vs return.
* Boundary-rule interpretation: eligibility for commons access; abuse definitions; prioritisation edge cases.
* Exposure tests: control/benefit/block mapping; consolidation; threshold bunching vs legitimate structure.

### Signatures

* Conflict concentrates on definitions, test procedures, and edge-case examples.
* Actors argue by “hard cases” and attempt to generalise exceptions.
* Pressure appears to rewrite definitions without restatement/bridging.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Interpretive disputes **MUST** route through published definition sets, adjudication notes, and appeal mechanisms.
* Any interpretive change with material impact **MUST** be treated as a definitional update: versioned, logged, bridged, and restated.
* If interpretive conflict produces exception stacking, escalation must treat it as drift/capture risk (P05 hard-stop triggers).

## B03.CH11.P03.S03 — Institutional conflict: authorities, audit findings, sanctions

Institutional conflict arises between governance bodies: regulators vs auditors, operators vs oversight, courts vs agencies, procurement vs integrity
units. This mode is dangerous because it can freeze enforcement or produce selective enforcement through turf war.

### Typical objects

* Disputes over audit scope, sampling, and access rights.
* Conflicts over sanction proportionality, enforcement priority, or interpretation of evidence.
* Procurement disputes over vendor lock-in, auditability clauses, and exitability requirements.
* Jurisdictional overlap: who has authority to decide, to halt, or to rollback.

### Signatures

* Sanction rate declines or becomes selective due to procedural conflict.
* Delays increase (enforcement latency↑) as bodies contest competence.
* Actors shop for forums (appeal routing becomes tactical sabotage).

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Institutional conflict **MUST** be resolved via pre-specified authority chains and arbitration/tribunal procedures; ad hoc political override is prohibited.
* Non-suspensive defaults **MUST** apply: core enforcement continues unless a defined stop condition is met.
* If audit access is obstructed (by institution or vendor), hard-stop rules must activate (fail-closed to auditable subset).

## B03.CH11.P03.S04 — Legitimacy conflict: mandate claims, representation, procedural fairness

Legitimacy conflict concerns **who is authorised** to decide and whether procedures are fair and inclusive. This mode is not reducible to technical
debate; it concerns representation, role structures, and consent. It becomes acute when groups feel burdened without voice, or when decision procedures
look technocratic or captured.

### Typical objects

* Mandate disputes: “this was not authorised” / “the mandate has expired” / “this violates the promise.”
* Representation disputes: panels/councils dominated by high-capacity groups; exclusion of affected communities.
* Procedural fairness: opaque deliberation, inaccessible participation, rushed decisions, or absent appeal routes.
* Emergency regimes: temporary powers normalised without sunset or ex post audit.

### Signatures

* Actors contest procedure more than substance.
* Compliance declines even if outcomes are beneficial (“unfair process” delegitimises).
* Narrative warfare intensifies: scapegoating, identity frames, distrust of metrics.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Legitimacy disputes **MUST** trigger procedural review: representation balance, access, timelines, disclosure, and appeal usability.
* Where legitimacy conflict is credible, re-authorisation procedures may be required (P06), but must not be used as a stalling tactic (non-suspensive defaults).
* Crisis measures must include sunsets, evidence publication, and ex post audits; otherwise legitimacy conflict hardens into rupture.

## B03.CH11.P03.S05 — Existential rupture: attack on non-negotiables (caps/commons/auditability)

Existential rupture is conflict that targets **constitutive rails**: abolishing or neutralising caps, converting commons into toll clubs, or breaking
auditability/contestability through black-box allocation, secrecy, or permanent exceptions. This mode is not resolvable by parameter compromise because
it changes what the system *is*.

### Typical rupture moves

* “Temporary” suspension of caps that becomes permanent via renewals.
* Black-box allocation regimes (ΔM or commons gating) justified as efficiency.
* Removal of replication rights and audit access; proprietary metrics become authoritative.
* Mass exception regimes that hollow out rails while preserving OBA language (washing).

### Signatures

* Exceptions explode; sanction rate collapses; reproducibility declines.
* Definition shifts occur without restatement and bridging.
* Enforcement becomes selective; dissent is framed as sabotage rather than contestable critique.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Existential rupture signals **MUST** trigger hard stops and recovery protocols (P05/P06): semantic freeze, rollback, reconstitution, and re-authorisation.
* If rupture persists or is explicitly chosen, the fork protocol **MUST** activate: classification, name label, and compatibility status (B03.CH07).
* Continued use of “OBA” labels under rail break **MUST** be treated as misrepresentation and corrected via relabeling and public disclosure.
