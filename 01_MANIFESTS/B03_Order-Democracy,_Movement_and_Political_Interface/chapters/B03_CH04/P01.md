# B03.CH04.P01 — Purpose and delimitation: commons as a democratically governed production and access system

This paragraph defines the **purpose** of the commons chapter: to specify the **governance rails** that make commons a durable,
non-extractive substitute for toll positions. Commons are treated as an **order object**: a production and access regime that must
deliver **open access within explicit boundary rules**, maintain **service minima** (ΔG), and remain **contestable and auditable**
over time. The chapter is not a sector policy manual; it is an interface specification for **who decides what**, **under which
constraints**, and **how drift/capture is detected and corrected**.

Delimitation is strict: the **economic classification rules**, measurement logic, and canonical definitions remain in the **Kernel
Spec / Book 1**; this chapter specifies how a commons is **governed** so that those canonical constraints remain operational under
democratic conditions.

---

## B03.CH04.P01.S01 — Commons ≠ state ≠ market: position in the order (substitution of tolls)

A commons is not a synonym for “public sector” and not a softer name for “market.” It is a distinct regime whose function is to
**substitute toll positions** by providing access to an essential service or infrastructure **without private gatekeeping** and
without dependence on rent extraction.

### Functional position
- **Not the state**: the state may fund, mandate, or enforce—yet commons governance requires explicit roles, decision logs, and
  contestability that prevent discretionary paternalism and opaque allocation.
- **Not the market**: pricing power, bundling, paid priority, lock-in fees, and take-rate extraction are constrained or prohibited
  because they recreate toll gates.
- **Order function**: commons reduce E by replacing claim-based access with **rule-based access** plus **maintenance discipline**.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any entity claiming “commons” status MUST specify the **substitution claim**: which toll positions are removed and by what access
  and maintenance mechanism.
- Any arrangement that relies on **private toll gates** (paid priority, bundling, lock-in fees, gatekeeping contracts) MUST be
  treated as **non-commons** unless requalified under a permitted non-extractive rule set.

## B03.CH04.P01.S02 — Delimitation vs Book 1: economic criteria and measurement rules remain canonical there

This chapter does not redefine P/E/C, caps, ΔM, or measurement methods. Those are canonical in the Kernel Spec and Book 1 (and where
relevant Book 2 for transition sequencing). The commons chapter is a **governance specification**, not an economic theory layer.

### Scope boundary
- **Canonical definitions live elsewhere**: terms and classification tests are imported by reference.
- **Local texts cannot override canon**: sector implementations may fill parameters but must not change definitions or invariants.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any policy or governance text in this chapter MUST reference canonical definitions (definition-set ID + version) rather than
  restating them as new local meaning.
- If an implementation cannot point to a canonical definition set for the terms it uses, it MUST be treated as **non-conform**
  until corrected (fail-closed against semantic drift).

## B03.CH04.P01.S03 — Core question: who decides on access, quality, maintenance, and investment

Commons succeed or fail on governance: scarcity decisions, service minima, maintenance backlogs, and capex priorities are the points
where capture and gating re-enter. The core design problem is therefore a decision-rights map with evidence and appeal rails.

### Decision domains (governance interface)
- **Access**: default openness; boundary rules; prioritisation under scarcity; user eligibility and exceptions.
- **Quality/service minima**: definition of minimum service levels; monitoring; incident thresholds.
- **Maintenance (ΔG)**: backlog measurement; ring-fencing; lifecycle discipline; repair obligations.
- **Investment (capex)**: project selection criteria; transparency of trade-offs; long-horizon commitments.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Commons governance MUST publish a decision-rights model covering access, quality, maintenance, and investment, including
  **who decides**, **what evidence is required**, and **how decisions are contested**.
- Allocation decisions that materially affect access or scarcity MUST be **contestable** (objection/appeal) and MUST be supported
  by **logs and measurable criteria**.

## B03.CH04.P01.S04 — Risks: capture, gating, maintenance decay, façade governance

Commons failure modes are predictable: they drift into gatekeeping, become vendor-controlled, or decay through maintenance neglect.
This chapter treats those risks as design constraints, not as accidental outcomes.

### Risk model (non-exhaustive)
- **Capture**: governance bodies dominated by vendors, insiders, or concentrated interests; procurement-driven dependence.
- **Gating**: informal exclusions; paid priority; bundling; “temporary” access restrictions that become permanent.
- **Maintenance decay (ΔG collapse)**: backlog growth; underinvestment; service minima violated; hidden deterioration.
- **Façade governance**: “commons” label with closed rules, black-box allocation, or non-contestable decisions.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- A commons MUST maintain **anti-capture rails** (disclosure, rotation, procurement exitability, audit-of-auditors) and MUST treat
  capture indicators (exception explosion, vendor concentration, sanction-rate collapse) as escalation triggers.
- If openness, contestability, or maintenance discipline cannot be demonstrated with evidence, the commons claim MUST be treated as
  **uncertain** and therefore **non-conform** until remediation.
