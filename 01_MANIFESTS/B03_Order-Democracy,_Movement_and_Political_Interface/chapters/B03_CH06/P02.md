# B03.CH06.P02 — Subject positions: who acts and from which structural position

This paragraph defines **subject positions** as the **structural roles** from which actors experience, interpret, and act upon
OBA order criteria. Subject positions are not identities or moral categories; they are **exposure profiles** and **incentive
bundles** shaped by where an actor sits in the flow-and-control topology (income/security, access to essentials, claim positions,
veto power, and institutional proximity). The purpose is to make coalition design **predictable and testable**: movement strategy
must be built on what groups can rationally sustain under timing asymmetry, shocks, and counter-moves.

Subject-position analysis is therefore a mapping exercise across the OBA test axes:
- **E** exposure (tolls, fees, claim stacks, lock-ins),
- **R/B** exposure (buffers, stability, essentials continuity),
- **caps** exposure (bindingness and exception politics),
- **commons/ΔG** dependence (access, quality, maintenance),
- **ΔM** interface exposure (allocation visibility, contestability, black-box risks).

---

## B03.CH06.P02.S01 — Workers/precariat: R/B interest, time/security, exposure to essentials

Workers and precarious earners are typically defined by **high R/B sensitivity** and **low tolerance for volatility**: they face
tight buffers, high exposure to essentials pricing, and limited ability to arbitrage shocks. Their structural interest in OBA is
usually strongest where OBA delivers:

- **Essentials continuity** via commons substitution (ΔG up, gating down).
- **E reduction** where toll positions hit daily life (housing-adjacent fees, platform take-rates, energy lock-ins).
- **Predictable enforcement** that prevents selective burdening (anti-scapegoat rails; non-arbitrary sanctions).

Key risk: **timing asymmetry**. If implementation front-loads visible costs (price shifts, compliance burdens, transition friction)
without immediate buffer protection, this position can flip into **backlash** even when long-run gains are large.

---

## B03.CH06.P02.S02 — Middle percentiles: housing/pensions, status-loss anxiety, swing dynamics

Middle percentiles often carry pivotal **swing-group** properties: they have more buffers than the precariat, but are heavily
exposed to **asset/claim regimes** (housing valuations, pensions, credit conditions) and **status narratives**. Their behaviour is
typically driven by:

- **Perceived stability of life projects** (home, retirement, education pathways).
- **Fear of downward mobility** under shocks or rapid policy change.
- **Ambivalence**: they benefit from E-reduction in services, but may fear cap politics or asset repricing.

Coalition relevance: this position tends to decide whether OBA remains **majoritarian-stable**. If R/B buffers are credible and
sequencing avoids sudden status losses, middle percentiles can stabilise an OBA mandate; if not, they become the main channel for
exception pressure, scapegoating, and regression to OOA reflexes.

---

## B03.CH06.P02.S03 — SMEs and the local economy: toll and lock-in sensitivity, administrative burden

SMEs and local businesses are structurally sensitive to **toll positions** and **lock-in rents** (platform take-rates, payment
stacks, energy and logistics chokepoints), but also to **administrative complexity**. Their OBA alignment tends to depend on:

- Whether OBA reduces **rent extraction** by dominant intermediaries (E↓ through anti-lock-in, anti-fee-stack enforcement).
- Whether compliance is **standardised and low-friction** (clear rules, predictable audits, minimal discretionary bureaucracy).
- Whether commons provisioning improves **input reliability** (infrastructure, skills, local services).

Failure mode: SMEs can be recruited into anti-OBA coalitions when OBA is perceived as “more paperwork” without visible reduction
in platform rents or overhead stacks. Movement design must therefore pair OBA enforcement with **simplicity and exitability**.

---

## B03.CH06.P02.S04 — Public professionals: execution, audit, care/education—capacity bottlenecks

Public professionals (executors, auditors, frontline care/education, regulators) sit close to the **operational spine** of OBA.
They are decisive because OBA is enforcement- and measurement-intensive: without competence and integrity, invariants collapse.
Their structural drivers include:

- **Capacity constraints** (staffing, tooling, training) that determine enforcement latency and audit coverage.
- **Role conflict risks** (revolving doors, vendor dependency, political pressure) that create capture pathways.
- **Professional legitimacy**: they can stabilise trust if processes are evidence-bounded and contestable.

Key requirement: movement infrastructure must build **personnel pipelines**, **professional integrity regimes**, and **tooling
exitability** so that execution does not become a vendor-run black box.

---

## B03.CH06.P02.S05 — Commons users and stewards: maintenance, access, conflict resolution

Commons users and stewards are defined by direct dependence on **ΔG quality** (maintenance, uptime, access fairness). Their core
structural interest is that the commons remain:

- **Open within boundary rules** (no stealth gating, no paid priority, no bundling lock-ins),
- **Maintenance-first** (backlog discipline, ring-fencing, transparent service minima),
- **Contestable** (complaint → evidence → remedy; anti-façade detection).

They are crucial for early detection of decay: commons governance failure tends to appear first as service deterioration, informal
gating, or “temporary” exceptions that become permanent. Steward positions must therefore be protected against capture and burnout
(rotation, disclosure, audit of governance).

---

## B03.CH06.P02.S06 — Top-tail positions: claim-holders, platform power, finance stack (counter-moves)

Top-tail positions are defined by holding **claim and veto power** over extraction channels: asset rents, platform intermediation,
finance stack privileges, and procurement control. In OBA terms, they often occupy the very structures that caps and E-reduction
are designed to constrain. Their predictable behaviours include:

- **Exception engineering** (carve-outs, thresholds, netting tricks, “temporary” deviations).
- **Narrative and expertise capture** (think-tank stacks, advisory panels, model opacity).
- **Jurisdictional arbitrage** (relocation threats, credit tightening, litigation saturation).
- **Vendor lock-in strategies** (black-box tooling, proprietary measurement, audit refusal patterns).

This is not a moral claim; it is a structural prediction: if OBA threatens durable toll positions, counter-moves will target the
movement’s weakest links—legitimacy, staffing, measurement continuity, and coalition cohesion.
