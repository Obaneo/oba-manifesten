# B03.CH09.P03 — Representation models: architectures and trade-offs

This paragraph specifies the main **representation architectures** available to an OBA-compatible democracy and the trade-offs they introduce under
adversarial conditions. The goal is not to endorse one constitutional form but to define **model classes** that can be combined, audited, and
constrained by OBA rails. Every model is evaluated on the same dimensions: (i) **capture susceptibility**, (ii) **capacity to bind decisions to order
criteria**, (iii) **evidence and appeal compatibility**, (iv) **fatigue/throughput**, and (v) **ability to handle constitutive vs parametric vs
operational domains without semantic drift**.

## Core principles

* **No perfect model**: each architecture has predictable failure modes; OBA requires explicit mitigation rails.
* **Domain separation**: constitutive decisions require stronger legitimacy and stricter procedures than parametric or operational decisions.
* **Auditability is gating**: any representation body that cannot be audited becomes a capture surface.
* **Contestability is legitimacy**: participation is legitimate only when challenge, correction, and restatement are possible.

## B03.CH09.P03.S01 — Electoral: parliamentary representation with order rails

**Electoral/parliamentary representation** is the baseline model: citizens authorise representatives to legislate and oversee the executive. Under OBA,
the critical requirement is that electoral institutions are **bounded by order rails** so that coalition cycles do not degrade caps, auditability, or
commons discipline through exception politics.

### Functional profile (electoral model)

* **Strengths**: broad legitimacy, scalable throughput, clear accountability cycles, capacity to integrate multiple policy domains.
* **Typical failure modes**: short-termism, symbolic legislation, exception stacking to satisfy coalitions, capture via funding/lobbying, narrative dominance over evidence.
* **OBA-specific binding**: mandates written in pillar goals + hard stops; definition-set pinning; compulsory dashboards and incident reviews; explicit fork labeling when deviating.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Parliamentary decisions affecting caps, ΔM channels, auditability, or commons access **MUST** be tied to published definition sets and evidence bundles.
* Electoral bodies **MUST** operate with exception discipline (sunsets + rollback triggers) and public registries.
* If parliamentary discretion can override auditability or measurement rules, the model is **non-OBA-compatible** unless a higher-order hard stop exists.

## B03.CH09.P03.S02 — Corporatist/professional: sector and professional councils (capture risk)

**Corporatist/professional councils** allocate representation by sector, profession, or stakeholder category (e.g., labour, employers, experts). They
can add competence and reduce transaction costs, but they are structurally vulnerable to **organised interest capture** and to turning representation
into licensed gatekeeping.

### Functional profile (corporatist model)

* **Strengths**: domain expertise, stable negotiation channels, faster technical adaptation, ability to encode standards in professional practice.
* **Typical failure modes**: cartelisation, insider bargaining, representational monopoly, exclusion of low-capacity groups, “expert” veto against caps and transparency.
* **OBA-specific risk**: councils can become toll positions themselves—controlling entry, defining metrics, or negotiating carve-outs.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Any council with influence over caps, pricing in essentials, procurement, or audit standards **MUST** have: funding disclosure, rotation, conflict rules, and audit-of-auditors.
* Council outputs **MUST** be logged, publicly rationalised, and contestable via appeal rails; closed-door bargaining is non-compliant.
* Councils **MUST NOT** have unilateral authority to grant exemptions; exceptions require sunsets, rollback, and external audit.

## B03.CH09.P03.S03 — Sortition/citizen panels: lottery selection for constitutive and evaluative tasks

**Sortition panels** select citizens by lottery to perform constitutive or evaluative tasks (e.g., reviewing mandate compliance, assessing fork proposals,
setting parametric priorities within rails). Sortition’s key advantage is reducing electoral career incentives and breaking professional participation
monopolies—while its key risk is competence, manipulation via framing, and fatigue.

### Functional profile (sortition model)

* **Strengths**: diversity of experience, reduced lobby targeting, legitimacy for difficult trade-offs, insulation from party branding.
* **Typical failure modes**: agenda manipulation (what they are asked), expert capture (who briefs them), overload, symbolic panels without authority.
* **OBA-specific use**: particularly suited for (i) **evaluative** functions (dashboards, incident reviews), and (ii) **constitutive** review procedures where impartiality is required.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Sortition bodies **MUST** have: clear authority scope, structured deliberation within test axes, balanced briefing protocols, and transparency of inputs.
* Recommendations **MUST** reference definition-set versions and evidence bundles; “gut feeling outcomes” are non-compliant for order claims.
* Panels **MUST** be coupled to an appeal/correction mechanism and cannot be used as legitimacy theatre without decision impact.

## B03.CH09.P03.S04 — Multi-stakeholder commons boards: users, operators, auditors, public

**Multi-stakeholder commons boards** govern commons services and infrastructures by representing (i) users/members, (ii) operators/maintainers,
(iii) independent auditors, and (iv) public mandate custodians. Their purpose is to keep commons **usable**, **maintained**, and **non-captured**,
with explicit boundary rules and contestability.

### Functional profile (commons board model)

* **Strengths**: tight feedback loops (quality and maintenance), direct stewardship accountability, capacity to enforce boundary rules locally, clearer linkage to ΔG metrics.
* **Typical failure modes**: board capture by operators or loud minorities, gating/exclusion, informal hierarchy, metric laundering, fatigue among member-representatives.
* **OBA-specific binding**: ΔG discipline, open logs, complaint/appeal rails, sanctions for gating and capture attempts, and rotation.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Commons boards **MUST** publish: boundary rules, maintenance plans, ΔG metrics, decision logs, and exception ledgers.
* Users/members **MUST** have contestable complaint and appeal rights with evidence (logs/metrics); “trust the board” is non-compliant.
* Operator participation **MUST** be counterbalanced by audit independence and user representation caps to prevent capture.

## B03.CH09.P03.S05 — Hybrid models: dual-key (citizen + commons + parliament) for critical decisions

**Hybrid architectures** combine representation models to reduce single-point capture and to align authority with domain expertise. The canonical OBA
hybrid is **dual-key (or multi-key)** governance: certain critical decisions require concurrence from multiple bodies representing distinct roles.

### Canonical dual-key applications

* **Constitutive rails**: changing definition sets, cap bases, auditability standards → requires citizen authorisation plus an independent evaluative body (e.g., sortition panel or judicial review).
* **Commons boundary rules**: major changes require both public mandate alignment and commons board concurrence, to prevent political opportunism and operator capture.
* **Emergency powers**: temporary authority requires parliamentary act plus independent audit trigger + sunset + ex post review.

### Trade-offs (explicit)

* **Strength**: reduced capture risk, improved legitimacy, clearer evidence discipline, fewer irreversible mistakes.
* **Cost**: slower decision-making, potential deadlock, more process load, higher need for explicit escalation and rollback.

### Conformance requirements (binding)

* Dual-key mechanisms **MUST** specify deadlock resolution (escalation timelines, arbitration, fallback to safer baseline) without expanding discretion.
* Any key-holder **MUST** be bound by evidence and auditability requirements; otherwise dual-key degenerates into dual opacity.
* For critical decisions (caps, auditability, ΔM channels, commons access), single-body authority **MUST** be treated as capture-prone unless compensated by hard stops and independent audit.
