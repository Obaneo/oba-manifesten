# B03.CH05.P03 — Powers and checks: who may do what, under which conditions

This paragraph defines the **minimum powers** required to execute OBA governance (inspection, evidence production, access,
enforcement), and the **checks** that prevent these powers from becoming an order-violating control stack. The core rule is:
powers are granted **for measurability and enforceability**, not for discretionary dominance. Every power must be bounded by
**scope**, **proportionality**, **logging**, and **contestability**, and must remain compatible with the prohibited-linkage rails
and privacy constraints. Where powers expand under crisis, they must do so under **time limits**, **public rationale**, and
**ex post audit**, with rollback as a default.

---

## B03.CH05.P03.S01 — Inspection and production powers: scope, proportionality, mandatory logging

Institutions must be able to **inspect** and require **production of evidence** sufficient to verify classification, routing,
exceptions, and sanctions. Without these powers, the regime becomes performative and capture-prone.

### Minimum powers (illustrative)
- **Inspection** of processes, records, and technical systems relevant to E/caps/ΔG/ΔM compliance.
- **Production orders** for documents, datasets, logs, model versions, calculation rule-sets, and contract annexes.
- **On-site / remote audit procedures** where necessary to validate custody, lineage, and operational reality.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Every inspection/production action MUST have a **defined scope** tied to a specific compliance question (no fishing by default).
- Actions MUST meet **proportionality**: least-intrusive means that achieves auditability.
- All actions MUST be **logged**: authority basis, requester, target, scope, timestamps, and resulting artifacts.

---

## B03.CH05.P03.S02 — Forensic access: systems, contracts, side letters, custody layers

OBA enforcement requires the ability to see through “paper compliance” into the **actual control and routing layer**, including
vendor tooling, subcontractors, and contractual side channels.

### Forensic access surface
- **Systems**: code/config, model cards where applicable, version pins, data lineage, execution traces.
- **Contracts**: master agreements, appendices, SLAs, pricing schedules, incentive clauses.
- **Side letters** and informal addenda that alter behaviour, carve-outs, or enforcement thresholds.
- **Custody layers**: intermediaries holding data/decision power (processors, platforms, consultants, escrow agents).

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Institutions MUST have legal/contractual rights to access evidence across the full custody chain.
- Vendor or contractor claims of “proprietary secrecy” MUST NOT block reproducibility; where necessary, controlled disclosure
  mechanisms must exist without nullifying audit.
- Missing access MUST trigger escalation: enhanced audit mode, procurement breach actions, or hard stops where the black-box risk
  breaks contestability.

## B03.CH05.P03.S03 — International cooperation: mutual assistance, evidence sharing, collection/seizure

Because extraction and claim-routing often cross borders, governance must support **cross-jurisdiction evidence cooperation**
without undermining domestic rights or creating surveillance overreach.

### Cooperation mechanisms
- **Mutual assistance** channels for evidence requests and coordinated audits.
- **Standardised evidence packages**: definition-set IDs, logs, computation artefacts, chain-attribution documentation.
- **Collection/seizure** cooperation where lawful and necessary, with tight scope and due process.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- International requests MUST be scoped to **defined compliance questions** and conform to proportionality.
- Evidence sharing MUST follow **minimum-necessary** disclosure and preserve chain-of-custody integrity.
- Cooperation frameworks MUST include contestability and review (no opaque cross-border enforcement by default).

## B03.CH05.P03.S04 — Override powers: conditions, time limits, public rationale

Override powers exist to address situations where ordinary procedures cannot prevent imminent order break (e.g., black-box
allocation, exception explosion, enforcement sabotage). Overrides are **exceptional** and must be tightly constrained.

### Allowed override triggers (examples)
- **Measurement blackout** or refusal to provide audit-critical evidence.
- **Cap erosion** through structural carve-outs or stacked exceptions.
- **Black-box allocation** that defeats contestability.
- **Acute crisis continuity** risks where delay causes irreversible harm.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Overrides MUST be time-limited and MUST include **sunset + rollback plan** at issuance.
- Overrides MUST be accompanied by a **public rationale** and a formal specification of scope and intended outcomes.
- Overrides MUST automatically trigger **ex post review** by independent audit and, where applicable, judicial scrutiny.

## B03.CH05.P03.S05 — Abuse prevention: audit of power use and sanctions for overreach

The same powers that enable enforcement can be weaponised. Abuse prevention therefore requires continuous monitoring and real
consequences for overreach.

### Abuse prevention controls
- **Audit of power use**: sampling and trigger-based audits of inspections, production orders, overrides, and seizures.
- **Appeal pathways**: targets must be able to contest scope, necessity, and handling of evidence (without sabotage defaults).
- **Transparency minima**: aggregate reporting on power use volumes, categories, outcomes, and error rates.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Overreach MUST be sanctionable at both individual and institutional levels (disciplinary, civil, and where relevant criminal).
- Illegally obtained evidence MUST be handled via defined remedies (exclusion, correction, restatements where series were affected).
- Repeated overreach patterns MUST trigger governance intervention: rotation, reconstitution, or restriction of powers until
  compliance is restored.
