# B03.CH03.P01 — Problem statement: electoral logic is insufficient for order decisions

Electoral legitimacy can select a direction, a governing coalition, and a bundle of priorities, but it does not by itself
provide a sufficient mandate for **order-relevant decisions**—decisions that affect **non-negotiables** (caps bindingness,
commons access/maintenance discipline, measurability/auditability, contestability) or that reshape the **allocation interface (ΔM)**.
Where electoral authorisation is treated as a blank cheque, OBA invariants become politically tradable, and the system becomes
structurally vulnerable to **capture**, **semantic drift**, and **regression**. This chapter therefore treats “mandate” as a
**typed, scoped, testable governance claim**: what can be decided, by whom, under which evidence standards, and with which
re-authorisation thresholds.

---

## B03.CH03.P01.S01 — Short-term incentives versus long-term invariants (caps, maintenance)

Electoral cycles reward visible, short-horizon wins and tolerate deferred costs. But OBA depends on long-horizon invariants that
are easy to underfund or hollow out without immediate political penalty: **caps enforcement** (which requires sustained detection,
qualification, and sanctioning capacity) and **maintenance-first commons stewardship** (which competes with headline projects).
A government can remain electorally “legitimate” while quietly degrading audit coverage, relaxing enforcement latency, and allowing
exception stacking—producing a delayed collapse in cap bindingness and commons quality.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Order-relevant commitments (caps/commons maintenance/audit capacity) MUST be expressed as **multi-cycle obligations** with
  explicit minimums (coverage, staffing, budgets, uptime/service minima where relevant) and MUST NOT be left to discretionary,
  year-to-year politics.
- Any decision that shifts resources away from caps enforcement or commons maintenance MUST be logged with rationale and impact,
  and MUST trigger review against the applicable mandate type (parametric vs constitutive).

---

## B03.CH03.P01.S02 — Majoritarian drift: how majorities can burden minorities and the future

Simple majoritarianism can legitimise outcomes that violate order-democratic constraints: selective enforcement of caps,
targeted exclusions from commons access, or crisis measures that normalise surveillance and profiling. Even when such decisions are
popular, they can impose concentrated burdens on minorities (rights and access) and on future generations (maintenance debt,
capex neglect, institutional erosion). OBA therefore treats certain protections—non-discrimination within boundary rules, contestable
allocation, prohibition of order-incompatible linkages, and maintenance obligations—as **democratic boundary conditions**, not
optional policy preferences.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Decisions that materially affect access, enforcement selection, or privacy/control MUST be contestable and MUST satisfy
  proportionality and non-discrimination rails; electoral popularity does not waive these requirements.
- Any action that increases future maintenance burden or weakens long-term enforceability MUST be treated as order-relevant and
  MUST face higher mandate thresholds (dual-key/supermajority where constitutive).

---

## B03.CH03.P01.S03 — Capture via mandate ambiguity: “technical” policy without contestability

Mandate ambiguity creates a predictable capture route: reframe order decisions as “technical implementation” (vendor tooling,
metrics, model thresholds, exception templates), then remove them from democratic scrutiny and judicial contestability. The result
is black-box governance by procurement and expertise stacks: definitions drift, dashboards become non-reproducible, exceptions
become normalised, and enforcement becomes selectively incapacitated—without a visible “political decision” ever being taken.
OBA treats this as a mandate failure: if a decision changes how the order is measured, audited, or enforced, it is not merely
technical—it is constitutive in effect.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any decision that changes **definitionsets, calculation rules, logging/audit access, exception machinery, or allocation scoring**
  MUST be classified as **order-relevant** and MUST be exposed to contestability (objection/appeal + independent audit).
- Procurement and tooling choices MUST preserve reproduceerbaarheid/replicability (second-source or shadow computation where needed)
  and MUST NOT create proprietary gatekeepers over measurement or allocation.

---

## B03.CH03.P01.S04 — Aim: tie mandates to testable order criteria

The aim of this chapter is to replace “mandate by narrative” with **mandate by testable criteria**. Mandates are decomposed into
types (parametric, constitutive, commons, ΔM, emergency) and tied to explicit requirements in the OBA test axes (E, caps, commons/ΔG,
R/B, ΔM). Legitimacy then becomes checkable: a decision is legitimate only if (i) it is taken under the correct mandate type,
(ii) it meets evidence and audit standards, (iii) it remains contestable without sabotage-by-procedure, and (iv) it preserves
non-negotiables or triggers re-authorisation/fork labeling when it does not.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Every order-relevant decision MUST cite: mandate type, applicable definition-set/version, evidence inputs, and the contestability path.
- If mandate type is missing or misclassified, the decision MUST be treated as non-conform until corrected (fail-closed on mandate).
