# B03.CH02.P05 — Mandate structure: how legitimacy is tied to order criteria

This paragraph defines how OBA links **political legitimacy** to the **order-impact** of decisions. Not every decision can be
legitimised by the same mandate mechanism. Where decisions can erode invariants (caps, E-reduction, commons access/maintenance,
auditability, contestability), legitimacy must be strengthened through **mandate typing**: electoral mandates for direction and
parameter choices, constitutive mandates for changes to rails, commons mandates for governance of shared systems, ΔM mandates for
allocation authority, and emergency mandates for crisis action under strict sunsets and evidence discipline.

The core principle is: **the stronger the potential for irreversible order damage, the stronger and more explicit the mandate
must be**—including who authorises, what evidence is required, what can be contested, and how rollback operates.

---

## B03.CH02.P05.S01 — Electoral mandate: direction, priorities, parametric choices

Elections are the default mandate mechanism for **directional politics**: goals, sequencing preferences, and parameter settings
that remain within invariant rails. Electoral legitimacy supports choosing between competing priorities, but it does not license
silent modifications of definitions, auditability, or non-negotiables.

### Legitimate scope (examples)
- Setting **thresholds, rates, and spending priorities** within pre-defined rails.
- Choosing **sector focus** and **sequencing** subject to hard stops and monitoring.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Electoral programmes MUST be translated into **test-axis commitments** (E/caps/commons–ΔG/RB/ΔM) with measurable targets.
- Electoral mandate MUST NOT be treated as a blank cheque for redefining canonical terms or weakening audit/contestability.

---

## B03.CH02.P05.S02 — Constitutive mandates: supermajority, referenda, or constitutional layers

Constitutive changes alter the **rails themselves**: definitions, bindingness of caps, core audit/contestability guarantees, or
commons boundary rules as invariants. Such decisions require higher-threshold mandates because they change the conditions under
which ordinary democratic choice operates.

### Legitimate scope (examples)
- Changing **cap formulas**, **exception non-stackability**, or **definition sets**.
- Altering **judicial review**, **audit independence**, or **contestability rights**.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Any proposed change that affects an invariant MUST trigger a **constitutive mandate route** (supermajority/special procedure).
- Constitutive changes MUST include a published **impact analysis**, **evidence plan**, and **compatibility status** (or fork label).

---

## B03.CH02.P05.S03 — Commons mandate: membership, representation, stewardship duties

Commons are governed production/access systems; legitimacy requires a mandate that reflects **users**, **stewards**, and the
public interest in continuity and non-discrimination. Commons legitimacy is not just “participation”; it is a defined set of
roles, authority boundaries, and stewardship obligations.

### Legitimate scope (examples)
- Setting **access rules** within boundary constraints.
- Approving **maintenance cycles** and **capex prioritisation** under maintenance-first discipline.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Commons governance MUST specify: **membership basis**, **representation model**, **term limits/rotation**, and **conflict-of-interest rules**.
- Commons decisions MUST be **logged**, **auditable**, and **contestable** (complaint → appeal → review).

---

## B03.CH02.P05.S04 — ΔM mandate: allocation criteria, transparency requirements, contestability

ΔM decisions allocate monetary space and therefore create high leverage points for capture. A ΔM mandate must explicitly bind
allocation to **criteria**, enforce transparency and auditability, and preserve contestability to prevent black-box distribution
or insider routing.

### Legitimate scope (examples)
- Defining **allocation channels** and **priority rules** (e.g., maintenance-first, RB buffers, productive credit).
- Setting **eligibility and routing rules** for funds with traceable lineage.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- ΔM authority MUST publish **allocation criteria**, **channel definitions**, and **decision logs** sufficient for replication.
- Any ΔM allocation mechanism MUST be **contestable** and MUST NOT rely on closed scoring/profiling without appeal.

---

## B03.CH02.P05.S05 — Emergency mandate: temporary powers, evidence discipline, exit conditions

Emergency action is sometimes necessary, but crisis politics is also the easiest vector for invariant erosion. OBA therefore
treats emergency mandates as time-bounded authorisations with strict **sunsets**, **rollback requirements**, and **evidence
discipline**, designed to prevent “temporary” exceptions from becoming normalised.

### Legitimate scope (examples)
- Temporary allocation overrides to maintain **essentials continuity** under shock.
- Crisis enforcement measures to stop acute extraction, subject to proportionality rails.

### Conformance requirements (binding)
- Emergency mandates MUST include: **sunset date**, **rollback plan**, **audit triggers**, and **exit criteria**.
- Emergency measures MUST NOT weaken auditability/contestability; if temporary limitation is unavoidable, it MUST be explicitly
  documented, time-limited, and compensated by enhanced ex post review (fail-closed where measurement is compromised).
