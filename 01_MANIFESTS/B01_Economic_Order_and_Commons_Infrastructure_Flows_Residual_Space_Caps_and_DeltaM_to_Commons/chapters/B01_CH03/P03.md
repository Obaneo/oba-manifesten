# B01.CH03.P03 — Burdens classification (L): what is “mandatory” and how to define it without drift

This paragraph defines the **L boundary problem**: what counts as “mandatory burdens” for the residual-space calculation, and how the definition is stabilized against semantic drift, lobbying, and Goodhart manipulation.

## B01.CH03.P03.S01 — L minimum set: housing, energy, basic insurance, basic mobility/communication (as categories)

L is defined as a **minimum category set** of unavoidable burdens required for baseline participation. The baseline categories are:

1. **Housing** — payments and charges required to secure baseline shelter and habitation under the defined minimum-quality standard.
2. **Energy** — baseline energy required for habitation and participation (heating/electricity baseline where applicable).
3. **Basic insurance** — mandatory baseline coverage required for participation (health and other mandatory schemes as defined).
4. **Basic mobility/communication** — baseline mobility and communication required to function in society (public transport or equivalent baseline; minimum connectivity/communication baseline).

These are categories, not a blank check. Each category requires:
- a boundary definition (what is included),
- exclusions (what is not included),
- and a method for local parameterization without redefining the category.

## B01.CH03.P03.S02 — “Mandatory” as a criterion: unavoidability, substitutability, minimum quality, local variation

A burden component counts as “mandatory” only if it meets all of the following criteria:

- **Unavoidability:** a reasonable household cannot avoid the burden without losing baseline participation or falling below minimum quality.
- **Substitutability:** there is no realistic lower-cost substitute available without clamp conditions (contract lock-in, discrimination, unreasonable travel/admin friction).
- **Minimum quality:** the burden corresponds to a minimum quality standard (not discretionary comfort upgrades).
- **Local variation rule:** where geography affects the minimum burden (e.g., heating needs, travel), variation is allowed only within declared parameter ranges and must be auditable.

If any criterion fails, the burden is not eligible for L and must be treated as discretionary or as a separate analysis object. If eligibility is ambiguous, classification must be conservative and the ambiguity must be disclosed.

## B01.CH03.P03.S03 — Reference budgets: how benchmarks are fixed (without lifestyle subsidy)

To prevent L from becoming “whatever people spend”, L categories use **reference budgets** that represent a minimum baseline, not a lifestyle subsidy.

Reference budgets must be:

- **normed to minimum adequacy:** defined as a baseline for participation and health/safety, not consumer preference.
- **versioned and published:** including category definitions, weights, and price sources.
- **independent of luxury signals:** luxury tiers and premium options are excluded by construction.
- **auditable and replicable:** external parties must be able to reproduce the benchmark within declared tolerances.

Where reference budgets depend on basket composition (housing types, energy baseline), the composition must be declared and stable across revisions, with explicit transition rules.

## B01.CH03.P03.S04 — Drift prevention: change procedure, version control, transition periods

Because L is a political and economic contest surface, changes require strict governance:

- **Change procedure:** updates to L categories, inclusion rules, or reference budgets require a formal proposal, published rationale, and impact analysis on R/B distributions.
- **Version control:** every change increments a version and is recorded in a changelog; indicators must carry the version used.
- **Transition periods:** changes that materially affect measured R/B require declared transition periods and, where necessary, restatements of historical series.
- **Exception control:** bespoke exceptions are disallowed. Only predefined parameterization and published revision cycles are allowed.

If drift indicators emerge (exception growth, widening ambiguity, repeated lobbying-driven boundary expansions), the system triggers a definition-stability review.

## B01.CH03.P03.S05 — Abuse/avoidance: labeling luxury as “mandatory” — mitigations and audit rules

A primary Goodhart pattern is to relabel discretionary consumption as “mandatory” to inflate L and justify interventions, or conversely to narrow L to cosmetically improve R/B. Both directions are incompatible.

Mitigations:

- **Hard exclusions:** premium tiers, luxury add-ons, elective upgrades, and preference-driven consumption are categorically excluded.
- **Anti-bundling rule:** if a provider bundles mandatory and non-mandatory components, the non-mandatory component is excluded from L and bundling is flagged as an E signal (lock-in / fee-stack behavior).
- **Audit checks:** sampling of L items, price-tier detection, anomalous regional spikes, and correlation checks with luxury markers.
- **Conservative treatment under opacity:** where bundling or pricing opacity prevents separation, the case triggers audit escalation; the system does not accept self-reported “mandatory” labels.

The rule is symmetric: L is neither a political dumping ground nor a cosmetic shrinking target. It is a bounded, versioned minimum burden definition that must remain stable and auditable over time.
